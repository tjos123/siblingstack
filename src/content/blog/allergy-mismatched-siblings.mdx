**A health-content note, read first:** This guide is about kitchen logistics and reading food labels correctly — not about diagnosing an allergy, deciding when to introduce an allergen, or managing a reaction. If your child has a diagnosed or suspected food allergy, your allergist or pediatrician is the right source for introduction timing, reaction management, and any individualized dietary guidance. Nothing in this guide should be used to make a decision about whether a specific food is safe for your specific child — that determination depends on your child's actual diagnosis and your medical team's guidance, not on general label information.

**Bottom line up front:** In a close-age-sibling household where one child has a diagnosed food allergy and the other doesn't, the biggest risk usually isn't the allergic child's own meal — it's cross-contact from the _other_ child's meal, sitting on the same table, touched by the same hands, or served from the same shared surface. All four companies covered in this cluster disclose _some_ allergen information, but none of them make identical promises, and none of the disclosures function as a guarantee against cross-contact. Reading exactly what each company does and doesn't promise — and building kitchen habits that account for the sibling sitting right next to the allergic child — matters more here than picking the "safest" brand.

## 1. The Regulatory Baseline: What Companies Actually Have to Tell You

U.S. food labeling law (the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act, later expanded by the 2021 FASTER Act to include sesame) requires that food labels clearly identify any of the **nine major allergens** — milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, sesame, and soybeans — when that allergen is an actual ingredient in the product. This is a real legal requirement, and every company in this cluster follows it for its ingredient lists.

What the law does **not** require is any disclosure about shared-facility or shared-equipment cross-contact risk. Statements like "may contain tree nuts" or "processed in a facility that also handles peanuts" are entirely **voluntary** — a company can choose to include this warning, choose to omit it, or provide more detail than the law requires, and there's no standardized threshold for what counts as safe enough to omit a warning. This matters directly for a sibling household: **the absence of a shared-facility warning on one child's meal doesn't guarantee the facility that made it never touches the allergen in question** — it may simply mean the company chose not to add a voluntary statement.

## 2. What Each Company in This Cluster Actually Discloses

## Little Spoon

Little Spoon states that its Babyblends purées are free from all nine major allergens. Its Plates line does not use nuts as an ingredient, with the specific exception of coconut, and the company provides menu filters allowing a parent to exclude specific allergens (gluten, dairy, nuts, coconut, sesame, and others) when selecting meals. At the same time, Little Spoon's own materials state that some products are made in facilities that also handle common allergens, and the company is explicit that its manufacturing partners follow allergen-control procedures designed to reduce cross-contact risk — not eliminate it. The company directs parents to review each specific meal's ingredient and allergen information before ordering, rather than relying on a blanket "allergen-free" assumption across the whole product line.

## Once Upon a Farm

As established in the companion sibling-milestone guide in this cluster, Once Upon a Farm discloses that its production facility processes tree nuts, egg, dairy, and soy, with stated cleaning protocols between product runs. This is a facility-level disclosure rather than an ingredient-level one — meaning a specific pouch or Meal Starter might not list any of those four allergens as an ingredient, while still being produced in a facility where that allergen is present in other products made nearby.

## Tiny Organics

As established in the companion Tiny Organics vs. Nurture Life audit, Tiny Organics' meals are described as free of most common allergens, but the company is manufactured in a shared facility that processes allergens, and its own materials note the brand is generally not positioned as a sole feeding solution for an infant with a severe, diagnosed allergy.

## Nurture Life

Also established in the companion audit: Nurture Life states its facility is free of peanuts and tree nuts, with the specific exception of coconut, and many of its individual meals are free of other major allergens (egg, milk, wheat, fish, soy, sesame) — but the company directs families to check the specific "contains" statement on each individual meal rather than relying on a facility-wide claim, and separately notes it may not be appropriate for a child with celiac disease specifically, which requires a stricter standard than general gluten-avoidance.

## The Pattern Across All Four

No company in this cluster claims to operate a fully dedicated, single-allergen-free facility for its entire product line the way some specialty allergen-free brands do. All four instead rely on a combination of ingredient-level allergen-free formulation, facility-level disclosure, and voluntary cross-contact warnings — which means **the meaningful comparison between brands isn't "allergen-free vs. not," it's which specific allergens each company is more or less transparent about, and whether that transparency covers the specific allergen your family needs to manage.**

## 3. The Sibling-Specific Risk: Cross-Contact That Has Nothing to Do With the Company

Even a genuinely allergen-free meal for the allergic child can become a risk once it's on the same table as the sibling's meal — this is a kitchen-logistics problem, not a manufacturing problem, and it's the part of this scenario that no company's allergen statement can address, because it happens after delivery, in your kitchen.

**Shared surfaces.** A highchair tray, a shared placemat, or a table surface that held the non-allergic sibling's peanut-containing snack minutes earlier can transfer allergen residue to the allergic child's plate even if that plate itself came from an allergen-free product.

**Shared utensils and hands.** A caregiver feeding both children in quick succession, or a sibling reaching over to "share" a bite, can transfer allergen residue directly — this is widely recognized as one of the more common real-world cross-contact routes in households managing a mixed-allergy sibling set, distinct from anything a food company controls.

**Simultaneous unpacking and prep.** Preparing both children's meals on the same counter or cutting board without cleaning between them re-creates the same cross-contact risk the food company's own facility disclosure is trying to describe — except now it's happening in your kitchen, where there's no equivalent voluntary warning label to catch it.

**Practical, logistics-only mitigations** — none of which require any product recommendation, and all of which are standard practice recommended broadly for households managing food allergies:
- Serve the allergic child's meal on a distinct, consistently used plate or tray, separate from the sibling's.
- Prepare and plate the allergic child's meal first, on a cleaned surface, before introducing the sibling's potentially allergenic food to the same space.
- Use separate utensils per child rather than a shared serving spoon moving between two different meals.
- Wipe down the shared table or highchair tray surface between the sibling's meal and the allergic child's, rather than assuming visual cleanliness is sufficient.
- Establish a household rule (developmentally appropriate to the older sibling's age) about not sharing bites or utensils with the younger or allergic sibling, revisited as the child is old enough to understand it.

## 4. Reading a "May Contain" Label Correctly

Because voluntary cross-contact statements aren't standardized, two different products' "may contain" labels can reflect very different actual risk levels, and there's no way to tell that from the label alone. A useful mental model for a parent reading these labels: **the presence of a shared-facility warning tells you the company is being transparent about a real manufacturing overlap; the absence of one doesn't tell you the overlap doesn't exist** — it may simply mean the company hasn't chosen to disclose it, since the law doesn't require them to.

This is exactly why every company disclosure above includes some version of "review the specific meal's ingredient list" rather than a single blanket claim — the individual meal or batch is the actual unit of relevant information, not the brand as a whole. For a household managing a specific, diagnosed allergy, contacting the company directly to ask about their specific facility practices for that specific allergen — rather than relying on the general marketing language on a product page — is the more reliable step when the stakes are meaningfully high.

---

## 5. When This Is a Kitchen-Logistics Question vs. a Medical One

A clear dividing line, stated plainly: **deciding how to physically separate two children's meals to avoid cross-contact is a kitchen-logistics question**, and the mitigations above apply regardless of which specific allergen or which specific company is involved. **Deciding whether a specific ingredient, product, or shared-facility risk is safe for your specific child is a medical question**, and it depends on your child's actual diagnosis, the severity of their allergy, and guidance from your allergist — informed by allergy testing and clinical history this guide has no access to and cannot substitute for. If you're ever uncertain which category a decision falls into, treat it as the medical question and involve your child's medical team rather than resolving it independently based on general label-reading practices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does "may contain" mean the product definitely contains trace amounts of that allergen?**
Not necessarily — "may contain" and similar voluntary statements indicate a possibility of cross-contact during manufacturing, not a confirmed presence in every unit; the statement reflects a company's precaution, not a guaranteed contamination level, and interpreting it for your specific child's risk tolerance is a conversation for your allergist.

**If a company doesn't list a shared-facility warning, does that mean the product is safe for my child's allergy?**
No — voluntary cross-contact disclosures aren't legally required, so the absence of a warning could mean the facility genuinely has no exposure to that allergen, or it could simply mean the company chose not to add a voluntary statement; this ambiguity is exactly why contacting the company directly for specifics matters more than relying on the absence of a label.

**Can I order allergen-free meals for one child and standard meals for the sibling from the same subscription account?**
Generally yes across the companies in this cluster — most allow filtering or selecting specific meals within a single order, meaning you can typically build one combined order with different meal selections for each child rather than needing entirely separate accounts, though always confirm this directly with the specific company for your specific allergen concern.

**Is it safer to choose the company with the most allergens disclosed as facility risks, since they seem the most transparent?**
Not necessarily — more disclosure reflects more transparency, not necessarily more actual risk, since disclosure is voluntary and companies vary in how cautious they are about adding warnings; the more reliable approach is checking which specific allergen your family needs to manage against each company's specific disclosure for that allergen, rather than ranking companies by total number of warnings.

**Should I keep my allergic child and non-allergic sibling from eating at the same table entirely?**
This guide can't make that determination for your specific child's allergy severity — for many families, standard cross-contact precautions (separate plates, cleaned surfaces, no bite-sharing) are sufficient to allow shared mealtimes safely, but for a child with a history of severe reactions, your allergist may recommend more stringent separation; this is a medical risk-tolerance question specific to your child's history, not a general rule this guide can set.

**Do any of these companies offer a dedicated allergen-free facility with no shared-equipment risk at all?**
Based on the disclosures reviewed in this cluster, none of the four companies claims a fully dedicated, single-allergen-free facility across its entire product line the way some specialty allergen-free-only brands do; each relies on some combination of ingredient-level formulation and facility-level allergen control rather than total facility exclusivity.

**How do I find out exactly what allergen precautions a specific company takes, beyond what's on their website?**
Contact the company's customer support directly and ask about their specific facility practices for the specific allergen you're managing — every company in this cluster provides a support contact for exactly this kind of ingredient- and allergen-specific question, and a direct answer from the company is more reliable than inferring risk level from general marketing language.

**My toddler wants to share food with their allergic younger sibling — how do I explain this in an age-appropriate way?**
This is a parenting and communication question outside the scope of a food-logistics guide, and how you approach it will depend on your older child's age and understanding; many families find it helpful to frame it simply and consistently ("this food could make your sibling very sick, so we don't share it") and to work with your pediatrician or allergist for age-appropriate resources if you want more structured guidance.
