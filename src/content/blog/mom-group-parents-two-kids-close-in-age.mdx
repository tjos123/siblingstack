Most new-parent groups form naturally around a single shared reference point — a due date, a birth month, a "new baby" class. That structure works well for a first kid. It works less well once you have two kids close in age, because the group's conversations, pace, and logistics assume everyone's managing one child, not the specific chaos of overlapping schedules, gear, and attention.

## Standard "new mom" groups aren't the wrong place — they're just incomplete

A general new-parent group is still worth joining for the newborn-specific conversations — feeding questions, sleep basics, postpartum recovery. What it won't cover well is the toddler-plus-newborn logistics that make up so much of daily life at this stage. Most parents in that kind of group end up needing a second, more specific community alongside it, not instead of it.

## Look for groups organized around the situation, not the age

Search terms like "close in age siblings," "Irish twins," "two under two," or your specific age gap in months tend to surface more targeted communities than generic "mom group" searches, which mostly return single-child content. Facebook groups, local parenting forums, and some hospital or pediatric-practice-run groups increasingly have subgroups specifically for parents managing overlapping-age siblings — worth asking your pediatrician's office directly if they know of one, since these often aren't well indexed by search.

## A specific national resource worth knowing

If local, situation-specific groups aren't turning up, Postpartum Support International runs free virtual and in-person groups for pregnant and postpartum parents across the country, including groups oriented around the emotional weight of this stage rather than just logistics. It's worth knowing about specifically because postpartum isolation is a recognized risk factor for perinatal mood and anxiety difficulties, and connecting with other parents is one of the more effective, accessible ways to counter that — not just a "nice to have" alongside the practical logistics support.

## In-person groups solve a different problem than online ones

Online groups are good for fast, specific questions — gear recommendations, "is this normal" reassurance, schedule troubleshooting from people in the exact same stage. In-person groups solve a different problem: actual physical support during a hard stretch, someone to sit with you while you manage two kids solo, or a standing weekly reason to leave the house. Most parents at this stage benefit from having one of each rather than treating them as interchangeable.

## Library and community programs sometimes work better than "mom groups" specifically

A structured toddler program — story time, a toddler gym class, a stroller-based fitness class, a babywearing meetup, a mommy-and-me program through a local faith community — often ends up functioning as an informal support group even though it's not labeled one, because you naturally end up next to other parents managing a stroller, a toddler, and an infant carrier at the same time. These venues select for parents in a similar logistical situation without you having to search for a dedicated group at all.

## Meeting someone is step one — following up is the part that actually builds the friendship

It's worth naming directly: standing next to another overwhelmed parent at story time doesn't create a friendship on its own, any more than one good conversation does. The parents who actually build a connection tend to be the ones willing to do the slightly awkward next step — asking for a phone number, suggesting a specific park meetup rather than a vague "we should get together sometime," and following through on that first plan even when it would be easier to stay home. Think of it less like making a friend and more like a low-stakes ask: propose one concrete thing (a Tuesday morning at the same park) rather than an open-ended invitation, since specific plans are much easier for another exhausted parent to say yes to than vague ones.

## If a whole group feels like too much, aim for one connection instead

You don't need a full group to get real value out of this. A single "person going through the same stage" — someone you can text a quick "is this normal" question to, or who understands without explanation why today was hard — provides a lot of the same support a bigger group does, with far less social effort required to maintain it. If you're naturally more introverted, or simply don't have the bandwidth for group logistics right now, this is a completely reasonable way to approach building support rather than a smaller consolation version of it.

## If you can't find one, a smaller informal group works just as well

A dedicated "two under two" group isn't always available locally, especially outside larger cities. In that case, a smaller, informal connection — even one or two other parents you met at a park or class who are managing a similar age gap — often provides more consistent, relevant support than a large but generic group where you're the only one dealing with overlapping-age logistics.

## What to actually let go of

- You don't need a perfect group match to get value from a community. Partial overlap (a group for toddler siblings generally, even without your specific gap) still beats no community at all.
- It's fine to be in a group mostly to read rather than post. Passive participation still counts as support, especially in a stretch where you have limited time to engage actively.
- Outgrowing a group as your kids' ages diverge from the group's focus isn't a loss — it's a normal part of this stage evolving, and it's fine to look for a new one as your specific needs shift.

## Common questions

**Where can I find postpartum support groups if I can't find anything specific to two kids close in age?**
Postpartum Support International maintains free virtual and in-person groups nationally and is a solid starting point when local, situation-specific groups aren't available — your pediatrician's office is also worth asking directly, since many hospital-affiliated groups aren't well indexed by a general search.

**I met a mom I liked at a class but we haven't talked since — is that normal?**
Very common, and it usually just means the follow-up step didn't happen yet, not that the connection wasn't real. Proposing one specific, low-effort plan (a set day and place) tends to work better than a vague "let's hang out" that's easy for both of you to let slide.

**Is it better to look for one mom friend or a whole group?**
Neither is more "correct" — a single reliable connection covers a lot of the same emotional ground as a group with much less social effort to maintain, and it's a completely valid approach if group settings feel like too much right now.
