An 11-month age gap is, mathematically speaking, the worst case for a Lovevery subscription — and also the best case, depending entirely on how you run it. Both kids move through developmental stages nearly in lockstep, which means if you buy a fresh box for both of them, you're buying the _same_ box twice within a year, with none of the "older sibling hands down to younger sibling" benefit that makes a 17-month gap so economical. But if you flip your thinking, that lockstep actually becomes the whole point: you don't have to buy two of everything. You just have to buy everything once, and skate the second kid just behind the first kid through the same stages.

That's where the "skip every other kit" strategy comes from. It's not an official Lovevery plan, and the company won't sell it to you in one payment, but it's a structural advantage hidden inside the subscription model that becomes available the moment you understand how the stage calendar, the shipping controls, and the resale valve actually work together. This guide walks through the whole strategy end to end — the stage math at an 11-month gap, the exact skip-and-delay mechanics in the portal, a real sample calendar, the honest edge cases where the plan breaks down, and a full FAQ.

## Why an 11-Month Gap Is the Trickiest Gap

The two-kids-under-two math in the [full financial breakdown](https://www.sibling-stack.com/blog/lovevery-subscription-worth-it-two-kids-under-two) assumes a gap of roughly 15-23 months, where stages hand off cleanly — older kid ages out just as younger kid ages in. At an 11-month gap, that neat handoff ladder collapses. Here's the actual stage timeline for two kids eleven months apart, using Lovevery's approximate two-month stage intervals in their first year:

:::table
Sibling #1 Age|Sibling #1 Stage (approximate)|Sibling #2 Age|Sibling #2 Stage (approximate)|Overlap Dynamic
0-2 mo|Stage 1 (newborn)|—|—|Single kid; no overlap yet
4-6 mo|Early exploratory|—|—|Single kid; no overlap yet
9-11 mo|Object permanence / grasping|0 mo (birth)|Stage 1 (newborn)|Opposite ends of the kit calendar — no overlap yet
11-13 mo|Crawling / early standing|0-2 mo|Stage 1 (newborn)|Sibling #1 entering kit #5-6 while #2 is still in newborn years
13-15 mo|Toddler-entry|2-4 mo|Early reflexive play|Overlap begins — #1's earliest toys start matching #2's needs
15-17 mo|Active toddler|4-6 mo|Early exploratory|Real overlap — both kids at active play stages at once
17-19 mo|Advanced toddler|6-8 mo|Object permanence / grasping|Full overlap — same skill-set window, months apart
19-21 mo|Pretend-play entry|8-10 mo|Crawling / object permanence|Overlap continues
21-24 mo|Language-driven play|10-12 mo|Toddler-entry|Overlap starts thinning as #1 outgrows hands-on manipulatives
:::

Read the overlap column carefully, because it explains the whole strategy. For roughly a **six-to-nine-month stretch**, from when sibling #2 is about four months old until sibling #1 is roughly two, the two kids are in stage windows that are adjacent enough that the same physical toys genuinely serve both — just not both at the same time, always. Sibling #1 plays with a toy at its "intended" stage, and sibling #2 picks it up a few months behind. The kit cadence stays identical; you're just running your younger child in a shadow queue one box behind your older child.

The catch, and it's a real one: at 11 months apart, "one box behind" still means both kids are in the same kit cycle almost simultaneously. The toddler doesn't hand down a Stage 4 toy that the baby is ready for six months later — the baby is ready for it the same season. That's exactly why the naive approach (subscribe for both kids, let them each have their own kit tier) is the most expensive way to run this gap, and why the skip strategy is built on letting one subscription's worth of boxes serve both timelines with a modest delay.

## The Three Levers That Make It Possible

Lovevery's subscription controls aren't designed for this strategy explicitly, but three features in the portal give you everything you need to run it. Here's what each one actually does, straight from the account tools.

**Adjust your shipping date.** Every kit in the subscription is assigned a planned shipment date based on your child's stage timeline. You can push a kit's ship date back (and in some windows, pull it forward) directly from the account page. For the skip strategy, this is the fine-tuning knob: instead of skipping a box entirely, you can delay it by several weeks so it arrives precisely when sibling #2 catches up to that stage.

**Skip a Play Kit.** The headline control. Skipping a kit defers that box forward to your next scheduled box, without charging you for it and without losing your place in the sequence. The policy requires a little advance notice (a few days before the shipment window), and skipping does not cancel the subscription — you just don't pay for that box, and the cadence continues from the following kit. This is the lever that makes "every other box" literal, since Lovevery will happily skip any box you'd prefer to defer.

**Cancel and reactivate.** If you want to pause for longer than a single kit cycle, you can cancel the subscription outright (no penalty, kit access preserved to what you've already received) and reactivate when you're ready — learning materials and stage-based order history are retained in the account, so reactivation can pick up where you left off. This is the "hibernate the subscription for 4-6 months while sibling #2 is still in the newborn window" move.

The honest framing matters here: skipping a box doesn't save you the full box price in a two-child family, because your younger kid will eventually reach that stage and you may want the toys back. What skipping saves you is **the purchase of a duplicate**. The whole point of the strategy is that sibling #2 picks up sibling #1's kit five to nine months after sibling #1 had it — so a skipped box for one kid is usually an unnecessary box for the other.

## When the Strategy Breaks (Read This Before You Commit)

The skip-every-other plan is elegant, and real families run it successfully — but it has three honest failure modes you should price into your decision before kicking it off.

**The both-kids-want-it-now bottleneck.** At an 11-month gap there will be stretches where both kids are the right age for the same toy simultaneously, and a single copy won't serve two toddlers fighting over it. The fixes are rotation (the loving-parent strategy of hiding the contested toy until the conflict passes), distraction (introducing a different high-value toy to one kid), or — occasionally — just surrendering and buying a second copy of a genuinely great toy. Any of these is fine; the failure mode is letting a single contested toy cascade into buying every subsequent kit twice.

**Developmentally-sliding second kid.** If sibling #2's development doesn't track the shadow-queue timeline — slow to hit a motor milestone, or racing ahead of it — your "one box behind" math drifts, and you'll find yourself needing a box you skipped. The mitigation is staying subscribed to a cadence rather than fully canceling, and using the skip/delay controls to pull boxes forward when needed. You lose a little of the savings, but not all of it.

**The sibling #1 2-year-old cliff.** Once sibling #1 passes roughly 24 months, stage needs diverge sharply between the two kids — sibling #1 moves into language, pretend-play, and social-emotional developments that don't neatly overlap a baby's motor-skill toys. The windows that made this strategy work close, and you'll naturally stop sharing the kit calendar. Plan your finances around that cliff rather than assuming the savings continue forever.

The sweet spot here is obvious: the hybrid lands inside 55-65% of the worst case's cost while still covering every developmental stage for both kids. The pure pass-down route is cheaper on paper but catches a real timing problem — because at an 11-month gap the second kid isn't "behind" the first kid's stage sequence, they're chasing it a few months behind, which is exactly the window where random gaps in the archive produce toy droughts.

A useful refinement: if your budget tolerates it, run the hybrid but **never skip a kit that contains a durable, multi-stage, open-ended toy** (think the wooden ring stacker, the object permanence box, the grasping toys). Those are the long-run pass-down assets your archive keeps paying for year after year. When in doubt, skip the kit weighted toward consumables, books, and single-stage play pieces instead — and check the [box-by-box condition audit](https://www.sibling-stack.com/blog/lovevery-two-under-two-box-by-box-audit) for exactly which items in each kit survive two-kid use and which don't.

## The Steps in the Portal, Step by Step

Assuming you've decided to run the strategy, here's the mechanical side — the exact account actions, in plain terms.

**To skip an upcoming box:**

- Log in to your Lovevery account and open the Subscription or Kits section.
- Go to **Account Settings** (or the equivalent "Manage Subscription" page).
- Find **Manage Subscription** and locate the next scheduled kit's shipment.
- Choose the **Skip** option and confirm it at least a few days before the scheduled shipping window (Lovevery spells out the cutoff on the page; respect it, or the box ships anyway).

**To adjust a shipping date instead of skipping:**

- From the same Account Management screen, open the box you want to reschedule.
- Use the **Reschedule** control to push the ship date forward (or pull it back) within the allowed range.
- Confirm the new date, and check the confirmation screen — the rescheduled date is also when payment for that box is processed, so make sure you want the new timing before you save it.

**To manage shipments from your phone:**

- Open the Lovevery app and log in.
- Go to the **My Lovevery** section and open account/subscription settings.
- Tap the box you want to manage, then choose **Skip**, **Reschedule**, or **Pause** as appropriate, and confirm.

**To pivot the strategy midstream (single biggest recommendation):** Every 2-3 months, recheck your younger child's actual stage against your shadow-queue expectations. If they're running ahead, pull skipped boxes forward; if they're behind, skip the next box rather than shipping it empty. The portal will happily accommodate either adjustment, and it's how the strategy stays living rather than stuck.

## Common Mistakes That Undermine the Strategy

**Skipping the wrong boxes.** Skipping a kit packed with durable, multi-stage toys is short-sighted — those are exactly the items your archive needs for the second pass. Skip consumables and single-stage play pieces first.

**Forgetting the resale valve.** If your age-gap plan eventually outgrows certain boxes (it will, around the 24-month mark), boxed, complete, un-drawn-on kits have real resale value on Lovevery's own Pre-Loved program and the secondary market. A clean archive is a small investment that pays a resale dividend later.

**Synchronizing both kids' shipping to the same date.** If both subscriptions (or both schedule lines in one account) ship on the same day, you've recreated the two-subscription cost structure to no benefit. Stagger everything intentionally.

**Skipping so aggressively that the younger child hits a pure toy drought.** A fully-paused subscription with a crawling infant and no suitable manipulative toys in the house produces a cranky baby and a re-purchase you didn't budget for. Keep at least a few current-stage items available at all times, even if they're a half-behind the "ideal" stage.

**Treating the portal as set-and-forget.** The skip-every-other cadence requires quarterly attention. Households that set it up and ignore it for a year tend to discover one kid quietly accumulating unneeded kits while the other goes bare.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Lovevery officially support skipping every other box?**
The skip and delay controls are standard subscription features Lovevery documents for any subscriber, but "skip every other box to run two kids on one cadence" is a family strategy built on top of those controls, not a formal program. You're using the tools as intended — you just need to manage the skipping yourself.

**Won't my younger child miss the "right" stage window for toys that get skipped?**
Yes, occasionally — but a subtlety of the strategy is that the _shadow-queue delay is the mechanism_, so a skipped box for one kid is usually a box the other kid doesn't need yet either. When development drifts, reschedule rather than skip, and expect to pay for a small number of extra boxes over the course of the year. That's the plan working, not breaking.

**Is the skip strategy cheaper than just buying the second kid a full subscription?**
At an 11-month gap, nearly always. The naive two-subscription route duplicates every box because both kids are near the same stages. The skip strategy converts that duplication into a ~50-60% cost reduction, though you carry the overhead of active calendar management.

**What if my kids' needs genuinely diverge (e.g., sensory differences, one kid ahead/behind)?**
Then you buy some duplicate boxes — but buy them selectively, targeted at the specific diverging items, rather than automatically subscribing for both kids. Targeted purchases cover maybe 10-20% of the dual-subscription cost.

**How do I explain the strategy to my partner so it doesn't turn into an argument?**
Show them the 18-month model table above and put the number in front of them: roughly $600-1,000 saved over the first year and a half by running one subscription's kit stream instead of two, at the cost of a quarterly moment of portal management, with the resale valve as the safety net if anything goes wrong. Most partners find that a fine trade.

**Does skipping affect my resale value if I eventually sell boxes?**
Skipping doesn't reduce the resale value of boxes you do receive — complete, clean kits hold their value. It does reduce _how many_ kits you can sell, because you own fewer. For most families that's a feature: you paid less and you sold less, and the discipline of owning only what you use is well rewarded.

## The Bottom Line

An 11-month gap is the hardest possible gap for the naive two-subscription approach and the best possible gap for the skip strategy, because it's the gap where duplicate stage timing is maximized. Run a single kit stream through both kids, skip every other box, reschedule and pivot quarterly as the shadow-queue drifts, and you can realistically get two kids fully kitted-out through their early stages at fifty to sixty percent of the two-subscription price. The whole system depends on you actually engaging with the portal's skip, delay, and reactivation controls — but they're straightforward, and the payoff for two under two is one of the best levers in the entire subscription.

If your gap is wider than 13-14 months, the same tools let you run a cleaner version of this exact strategy with even less overlap management — and if it's narrower, the [single-box, sibling-passing approach in the full sibling math breakdown](https://www.sibling-stack.com/blog/lovevery-subscription-worth-it-two-kids-under-two) is the better first read. Either way, the principle is identical and worth internalizing: with two kids under two, the second subscription is a tax on attention, and the skip-every-other plan is the way around it.
