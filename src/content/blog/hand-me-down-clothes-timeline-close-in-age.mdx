With a bigger age gap, hand-me-downs are simple: clothes sit in storage for a couple of years, then come back out. With a small gap, the math is trickier — your younger child can catch up to a recently-worn size faster than you'd expect, which means the usual "box it up and forget it" approach often doesn't work.

## Why the standard hand-me-down approach breaks down

The normal assumption behind hand-me-downs is that there's enough time between kids for clothes to be packed away and forgotten until needed again. With roughly a year or less between your kids, that gap often doesn't exist — your younger child may need the next size up before you've even finished using the current one with your older child, especially during the rapid early-months sizing changes (newborn through 6 months, where sizes can turn over every 4-8 weeks).

## A more realistic system for a small age gap

**Sort by size, not by which kid wore it, the moment something's outgrown.** Rather than boxing items away by "this was [older child]'s," sort directly into size-labeled bins as soon as something's outgrown. With a small gap, you're likely to need that exact size again within months, not years, so anything that adds friction to finding it again costs you real time later.

**Expect overlap, not sequence, in the early months.** During the newborn-to-9-month range especially, it's common for both kids to need overlapping sizes at the same time for a while, particularly around seasonal transitions — don't assume a strict hand-down sequence will work cleanly here.

**Track season alongside size.** A 12-month size onesie handed down mid-winter may be the wrong weight of fabric for your younger child's 12-month stage if that lands in summer instead. Label storage bins with both size and a rough season note, not size alone, to avoid handing down a snowsuit in July.

**Keep a small "transition" bin instead of fully boxing things away.** Rather than sealing outgrown clothes into long-term storage, a more accessible bin or shelf for "recently outgrown, likely needed again soon" tends to save real time with a small age gap, since the turnaround is so much faster than standard hand-me-down planning assumes.

## What's usually worth buying new instead of relying on hand-me-downs

**Shoes**, consistently — foot shape and growth rate vary enough between siblings that a hand-me-down shoe fitting in length doesn't guarantee a good fit in width, and ill-fitting shoes affect a toddler's walking development in a way loose clothing doesn't.

**Anything with significant wear already**, since a small age gap means less recovery time between uses — fabric that was already getting thin after one kid often doesn't survive a second round as well as it would after a longer storage gap.

**Going-home outfits and other one-time-wear sentimental items**, less because they won't fit and more because parents often want each kid to have their own version of these specific pieces, sizing aside.

## A practical sizing buffer that helps with small gaps specifically

Because your younger child may need a size faster than expected, it often helps to buy slightly ahead — the next size up — rather than waiting to see if a hand-me-down will arrive in time. The cost difference is usually small, and it removes the scramble of realizing mid-week that the right size isn't actually available yet from the older sibling's outgrown pile.

## The bottom line

Hand-me-downs absolutely still save money with a small age gap — the savings just require a slightly different system than the "box it and wait a couple years" approach that works for bigger gaps. Sort by size and season immediately, keep recently-outgrown items more accessible than fully stored, and buy ahead on sizing when in doubt rather than counting on a hand-me-down arriving exactly on time.
