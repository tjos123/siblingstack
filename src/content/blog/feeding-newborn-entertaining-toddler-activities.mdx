A newborn feed can take anywhere from 10 to 40 minutes, several times a day — and if you're the only adult around, that's a lot of time your toddler needs to be safely and happily [occupied](/blog/feeding-schedule-two-different-ages) without your hands. The activities that actually work in this window share three traits: almost no setup, almost no supervision beyond glancing over, and enough novelty or engagement to hold attention longer than two minutes. (For the same "give the toddler a real job" approach at the other two pressure points of the day, see [solo bedtime](/blog/solo-bedtime-2-under-2-alone) and [stopping toddler nursery incursions during naps](/blog/stop-toddler-waking-sleeping-baby).)

Here are ten that consistently work, organized by how much prep they need.

## 1. The "feeding basket" (zero prep after setup)

Fill a basket with 4–6 toys that are *only* available during feeding time — nothing the toddler has free access to otherwise. The novelty of "special basket toys" does more work than the toys themselves; rotating the contents every week or two keeps it fresh without buying anything new. Keep it within toddler's reach but out of daily circulation.

**Best for:** Ages 12 months and up. **Attention span:** 10–20 minutes.

## 2. Sticker sheets on a clipboard

A cheap sticker sheet and a clipboard (or just a piece of paper on the floor) can occupy toddlers well past what seems reasonable for stickers. The peeling motion itself is engaging fine-motor practice, which is part of why it holds attention so well.

**Best for:** Ages 18 months and up (younger toddlers may need pre-loosened stickers). **Attention span:** 10–15 minutes.

## 3. Water painting on the patio or bath tile

A cup of water and a paintbrush "paints" on concrete, patio stone, or bathroom tile — the water darkens the surface temporarily, then evaporates, so there's no cleanup and no mess risk. This works especially well if your feeding chair has a view of the patio or you can feed near the bathroom.

**Best for:** Ages 18 months and up. **Attention span:** 15–25 minutes.

## 4. A muffin tin + small objects sorting game

An old muffin tin with pom-poms, large buttons, or dry pasta to sort by color into each cup. Simple sorting tasks like this tend to hold toddler focus well because there's a clear, achievable "finish line" they can see.

**Best for:** Ages 15 months and up (supervise closely if objects are a choking risk for your toddler's age). **Attention span:** 10–15 minutes.

## 5. Painter's tape "roads" on the floor

A few strips of painter's tape stuck to the floor in loops and lines instantly becomes a road for toy cars, or a path to walk/hop along. It removes easily without damaging most floors, and takes under a minute to set up once you have a roll on hand.

**Best for:** Ages 18 months and up. **Attention span:** 10–20 minutes.

## 6. A short screen show, used intentionally

There's no need to feel guilty about a 15–20 minute show during a feed — used consistently at the same time each day, it becomes a predictable part of the routine rather than a fallback, and predictability is what makes toddlers settle into it quickly rather than negotiating.

**Best for:** Ages 12 months and up (per your own screen-time comfort level). **Attention span:** 15–25 minutes (length of the show).

## 7. Busy bag: envelope stuffing

Old envelopes and a stack of index cards or junk mail — the toddler's "job" is stuffing cards into envelopes and "sealing" them (a lick-free glue stick or just tucking the flap works fine). This mimics an adult task, which many toddlers find intrinsically motivating.

**Best for:** Ages 20 months and up. **Attention span:** 10–15 minutes.

## 8. A "feed the animals" sorting bin

Stuffed animals lined up with a bowl of pom-poms, cotton balls, or dry cereal as "food" to feed each one. This combines pretend play with a fine-motor sorting task, which tends to extend engagement longer than either alone.

**Best for:** Ages 15 months and up. **Attention span:** 10–20 minutes.

## 9. Bubble wrap stomping

A sheet of bubble wrap taped to the floor gives a sensory, noisy, physically active option for toddlers who don't settle into quiet tasks easily. It's loud, which is a feature here — active, noisy play at a safe distance from the baby is often easier to manage than trying to force a wiggly toddler into quiet play.

**Best for:** Ages 12 months and up. **Attention span:** 5–15 minutes (good for restless days).

## 10. Read-to-the-baby "job"

Hand the toddler a board book and ask them to "read" it to the baby (or to a stuffed animal, if the baby is in a spot the toddler can't easily reach). This reframes the feeding window as a shared activity rather than a time the toddler is excluded from, which tends to reduce jealousy-driven interruptions — the same jealousy dynamic our [sibling jealousy](/blog/sibling-jealousy-close-age-gap) guide covers.

**Best for:** Ages 18 months and up. **Attention span:** 5–10 minutes, but often repeats itself with a new book.

## How to rotate these so they keep working

The biggest mistake with feeding-time activities is over-relying on one option until it stops working. A simple rotation — 2–3 different activities across a day, refreshed weekly — keeps novelty high without needing new supplies constantly. Keep a small "feeding time only" bin near your usual feeding spot so nothing needs to be located mid-feed.

## Safety note

For activities involving small objects (buttons, pom-poms, dry pasta), assess based on your specific toddler's age and mouthing habits — some toddlers are well past mouthing objects by 15 months, others aren't. When in doubt, choose larger-object versions (large pom-poms, jumbo buttons) or supervise more closely during that activity.

## FAQ

**What if my toddler still interrupts constantly no matter what activity I offer?**
Try setting up the activity a minute or two *before* you start the feed, so the toddler is already engaged when your hands become unavailable, rather than starting the activity right as you sit down.

**Is it bad to use the same activity every single feeding?**
It's not harmful, but most toddlers lose interest in a repeated activity within a few days; rotating 2–3 options tends to hold attention longer over time.

**How do I handle a toddler who's too young (under 12 months) for most of these?**
For a toddler under 12 months during a sibling's newborn feed, playpens, high chairs with snacks, or a few minutes of supervised floor time near you tend to work better than activity-based options, which usually need more independent play skill than very young toddlers have yet.
