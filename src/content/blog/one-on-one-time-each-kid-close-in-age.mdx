When two kids are far apart in age, one-on-one time often happens naturally — different bedtimes, different activities, different schools. When they're close together, almost everything defaults to group mode, and individual time has to be built deliberately or it simply doesn't happen. This isn't a sign you're doing anything wrong; it's just a structural feature of a small age gap that's worth planning around rather than feeling guilty about.

## Why this is harder, not just busier, with a small age gap

With a bigger gap, the older kid's independence creates natural pockets of one-on-one time with the younger one, and vice versa. With a small gap, both kids are often at similarly demanding stages simultaneously — both need physical care, both want your attention at the same moments, and neither is independent enough to occupy themselves while you focus on the other. The math isn't "twice the kids," it's "twice the kids, at the same level of need, at the same time," which is a meaningfully harder scheduling problem.

## What actually works, in practice

**Short and consistent beats long and occasional.** Ten predictable minutes most days does more for a kid's sense of security than an hour-long outing once every few weeks. Kids — especially toddlers — build their sense of "do I get my own time with this parent" from pattern and predictability, not from the size of any single instance.

**Use natural splits in the day rather than trying to manufacture new ones.** If you have two caregivers in the house, even briefly (a partner home from work, a grandparent visiting), that's often the easiest moment to split — one kid with each adult — rather than waiting for a special occasion. If you're managing solo most of the time, look for windows when one kid is napping or otherwise occupied to give the other kid uninterrupted attention, even briefly.

**Let the "special" activity be ordinary.** One-on-one time doesn't need to be an outing or a planned activity — folding laundry together, narrating what you're doing while you cook, or just sitting with one kid while the other naps all count. The value is in the undivided attention, not the activity itself.

**Rotate deliberately if you have a partner or co-caregiver, rather than defaulting to the same pairing.** It's common for one parent to gravitate toward the easier-to-manage kid (often the older one, since they need less physical care) while the other parent ends up mostly with the baby. Naming this out loud and swapping periodically prevents an unintentional pattern where one parent barely gets solo time with one of the kids.

**Track it, at least loosely.** Without some way of seeing the pattern, it's easy to lose track of which kid got more individual time this week — intentions are good, but memory under sleep deprivation is not a reliable system. Even a rough mental note ("I had alone time with the toddler twice this week, the baby zero times because they're easier to fold into group time") is more useful than assuming it's balancing out on its own.

## What to let go of

You will not achieve perfectly equal one-on-one time, day to day or even week to week, especially in the early months when a newborn's needs are simply more constant and less predictable than a toddler's. The goal isn't a perfect 50/50 split — it's making sure neither kid goes long stretches without any individual attention at all, and noticing if that starts happening so you can course-correct.
