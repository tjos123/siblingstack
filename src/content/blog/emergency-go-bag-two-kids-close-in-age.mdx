Most emergency preparedness checklists have a single line item for "kid supplies" — as if one bag of diapers and a few snacks covers it. With two kids close in age, you're often packing for two different diaper sizes, two different feeding needs, and two very different levels of self-sufficiency, all of which has to fit in something you can actually carry while also carrying or holding both kids.

## Build the bag around what you can physically carry, not what would be nice to have

The starting constraint isn't "what might we need" — it's "what can one adult carry while also managing two kids in an actual emergency." That means prioritizing weight and bulk reduction over completeness. A lean bag you can grab and move with beats a comprehensive one that's too heavy to actually take when it matters.

## Water and food belong in this bag too, not just diapers

It's easy for a "kid go-bag" to become entirely about diapering and comfort items and skip the basics that apply to the whole household. Standard emergency planning guidance calls for at least one gallon of water per person per day, for at least three days, plus non-perishable food for the same window — and that standard applies to your toddler and infant too, scaled down (pouches, dry cereal, shelf-stable snacks for the toddler; extra formula or a way to prepare it without power for the infant). Water in particular is easy to underpack for kids specifically, since it's heavy and tempting to skip in favor of lighter items — it shouldn't be the thing you cut for weight.

## The plan matters as much as the bag

A packed bag solves the "what do we grab" problem, but it doesn't solve the "what if we're not all together when something happens" problem, which is worth planning for separately. A basic family communication plan includes: an out-of-town contact every family member (and any regular caregiver) knows to call, since a shared regional emergency can make local calls harder to get through than long-distance ones; a specific meeting spot near home and a second one further away in case the neighborhood itself isn't accessible; and, for your toddler specifically, starting to teach them their own name, your name, and a phone number they can eventually recite, even before they can reliably do it under stress. None of this needs to be elaborate — it needs to exist and be reviewed occasionally, the way you'd check the bag itself.

## Pack by kid, then combine into one grab-and-go bag

Similar to a diaper bag setup, organizing supplies by kid first — rather than by category — means you're not searching through mixed supplies under stress:

- **Infant section:** diapers (aim for at least 2–3 days' worth), formula or pumped milk if applicable with a way to prepare it without power, a few premade bottles if that's part of your routine, a couple of onesies
- **Toddler section:** a couple of diapers or pull-ups if still needed, a change of clothes, a comfort item (this matters more in an emergency than it might seem — familiar objects help regulate a stressed toddler)
- **Shared section:** a first aid kit, water and shelf-stable food for several days, a flashlight, a whistle (useful for a child to signal for help if separated, and simpler for a toddler to use than shouting), a battery pack, and copies of both kids' vaccination records and any medication info

## Documents and cash you'll actually need, sealed and ready

Beyond medical records, a small waterproof pouch with copies of each family member's ID, your insurance information, and each kid's birth certificate saves real time if you need to prove identity, seek medical care, or file a claim somewhere unfamiliar — originals can stay secured at home, but copies belong in the bag itself. A small amount of cash in smaller bills is worth including too, since card readers and ATMs may not work during a widespread outage, and it's one of the more commonly missed items on family go-bags built primarily around kid supplies.

## Give each kid their own ID card, not just a shared document folder

With two kids at very different levels of verbal ability, a small card for each of them — name, your name and phone number, any allergies or medical needs, an out-of-town contact — is worth keeping in the bag and, ideally, on the toddler directly during an actual event (clipped to clothing or in a pocket). This matters more with two kids than one, since a toddler and an infant getting briefly separated from you or from each other in a chaotic situation is a realistic scenario a single "family" document doesn't fully cover.

## Comfort items do real logistical work, not just emotional work

It's tempting to skip a stuffed animal or blanket in favor of more "practical" supplies, but a familiar comfort item genuinely reduces a toddler's distress in an unfamiliar or stressful situation, which in turn makes the toddler easier to manage while you're also handling an infant and whatever the emergency actually requires. Treat it as functional gear, not a nice-to-have.

## Store the bag somewhere both accessible and out of daily rotation

The bag needs to be reachable in seconds, but it also can't be the bag you dip into for regular outings — a go-bag that's been raided for a normal Tuesday trip is a go-bag with missing diapers when you actually need it. A dedicated spot (a closet shelf, a spot by the door reserved only for this) with a monthly reminder to check and restock keeps it both accessible and reliably full. If you're regularly in the car with both kids, a smaller secondary version in the vehicle is worth considering too, since a car-based emergency doesn't guarantee you're near the main bag at home.

## Update it as fast as the kids grow

This is the piece most families let lapse — a bag packed when the infant was newborn-sized is badly out of date six months later. Diaper sizes, clothing sizes, and feeding needs change quickly at this stage. A recurring check-in, tied to something you'll actually remember (the first of every month, alongside a smoke detector battery check), keeps the bag realistic rather than technically present but practically useless.

## What to actually let go of

- You don't need a fully stocked bag for every conceivable scenario. A lean bag covering the first 24–48 hours is a realistic and sufficient standard for most household emergency planning.
- Perfect organization isn't required. A bag that's roughly right and easy to grab beats a meticulously organized one you haven't gotten around to finishing.
- It's normal for this to sit unfinished on your to-do list for a while. Getting even a basic version assembled is meaningfully better than none at all — it doesn't need to be complete to be worth having.

## Common questions

**How much water should actually be in a kid-specific go-bag?**
The standard guidance is one gallon per person per day for at least three days, and that applies to kids too, scaled to what they'll realistically drink — it's one of the most commonly underpacked items because of its weight.

**What documents should actually be in the bag versus staying at home?**
Copies, not originals: IDs, insurance information, and birth certificates in a small waterproof pouch. Keep the originals secured separately at home rather than putting them at risk in a bag that's meant to be grabbed quickly.

**Should my toddler carry any identifying information themselves?**
Yes, especially alongside an infant sibling — a simple card or tag with their name, your contact information, and any allergies or medical needs is a reasonable precaution given how easily a toddler and an infant could become briefly separated from you in a chaotic situation.
