If your toddler and newborn are going to share a room — whether by choice or because your home doesn't have a spare — the setup matters more than it would for two kids further apart in age, because a toddler's noise and a newborn's sleep needs can genuinely interfere with each other if the room isn't arranged thoughtfully.

## Start with the official safe sleep guidance for the newborn, non-negotiably

Regardless of room-sharing arrangements with an older sibling, current safe sleep guidance from pediatric health organizations recommends a baby sleep on a firm, flat surface, on their back, with nothing else in the crib or bassinet — no blankets, pillows, or soft bedding — generally for at least the first six months, and that the baby's sleep space be in the same room as a parent or caregiver for at least part of that window, not necessarily the same room as a sibling. It's worth reading current guidance directly from the American Academy of Pediatrics or your pediatrician, since this is updated periodically and matters too much to go by memory or older advice. The toddler-sharing question below is separate from, and secondary to, this baseline.

## Physical layout that actually works

**Put the newborn's sleep space closest to the door, the toddler's furthest.** This sounds minor but matters in practice — it means checking on or removing the newborn at night doesn't require walking past or near the toddler's bed, reducing the chance of waking them.

**Use a sound machine for each kid, not one shared unit.** A single sound machine in the middle of the room helps mask some noise for both kids, but two separate units — each positioned near its own kid — does a meaningfully better job of preventing one kid's noise from reaching the other, especially for a toddler who talks or stirs in their sleep.

**Blackout curtains help the toddler's nap schedule hold steady even as the newborn's nighttime patterns disrupt the room.** Toddlers are more light-sensitive in terms of sleep cue timing than newborns are in the first months, so protecting their light environment specifically tends to have an outsized payoff.

**Consider a temporary room swap instead of a shared room, if you have any spare space at all** — even a converted office or a pack-and-play in your own room for a few months. Many families plan for permanent room-sharing from day one when a temporary arrangement (newborn in the parents' room initially, as official guidance recommends anyway) followed by a later, more gradual room-share transition once the newborn's sleep is more predictable, ends up working better for everyone's sleep in the short term.

## Handling the transition if you do share a room from early on

**Expect more night wakings for the toddler initially, and have a plan that isn't "give up and let them in your bed every time."** Some disruption during the adjustment period is normal; consistency in how you respond (a brief, boring resettling routine rather than an engaging one) tends to shorten the adjustment window.

**A toddler's nightlight needs and a newborn's safe sleep needs aren't in conflict, but check your specific products.** A dim, warm-toned nightlight is generally fine in a shared room; just confirm it's not positioned to shine directly into the bassinet or crib, and that nothing battery-operated or with cords is within reach of either sleep space.

## When it's genuinely not working

If after several weeks both kids are consistently sleeping worse in a shared room than either did separately, it's reasonable to revisit the arrangement — room-sharing siblings is a logistics choice, not a developmental requirement, and reversing course isn't a failure if the data (consistently worse sleep for one or both kids) says it's not working for your family.
