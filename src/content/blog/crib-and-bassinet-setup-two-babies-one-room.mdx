If your toddler and newborn are going to share a room — whether by choice or because your home doesn't have a spare — the setup matters more than it would for two kids further apart in age, because a toddler's noise and a newborn's sleep needs can genuinely interfere with each other if the room isn't arranged thoughtfully. Here's a practical layout, a safety baseline, and a transition plan that works.

:::section
section-safe-sleep|1. Safe Sleep Guidance Comes First
badge:🛡️ Safe Sleep Standard
badge:👶 Ages 0–6 Months
Regardless of room-sharing arrangements with an older sibling, current safe sleep guidance from pediatric health organizations recommends a baby sleep on a firm, flat surface, on their back, with nothing else in the crib or bassinet — no blankets, pillows, or soft bedding — generally for at least the first six months. Crucially, those organizations recommend the baby's sleep space be in the same room as a parent or caregiver for at least part of that initial window — not necessarily in the same room as a sibling. It's worth [reading current guidance directly](/blog/car-seat-two-different-sizes) from the American Academy of Pediatrics or your pediatrician, since it's updated periodically and matters too much to go by memory or older advice. The toddler-sharing question below is separate from, and secondary to, this baseline.

:::warning
**⚠️ Temporary Room Swap:** Many families plan for permanent room-sharing from day one. But if you have any spare space at all — even a converted office or a pack-and-play in your own room for a few months — a temporary arrangement (newborn in the parents' room initially, as official guidance recommends anyway) followed by a gradual room-share transition once the newborn's sleep is more predictable tends to work significantly better for everyone's sleep in the short term.
:::

:::section
section-layout|2. Physical Layout That Actually Works
badge:📐 Room Architecture
- **Newborn closest to the door, toddler furthest away:** Checking on or removing the newborn at night won't require walking past or near the toddler's bed, drastically reducing accidental wake-ups.
- **Use two separate sound machines, not one shared unit:** A single central unit helps mask some noise, but two units — each positioned near its own kid — do a meaningfully better job of preventing one kid's noise from reaching the other, especially for a toddler who talks or stirs in their sleep.
- **Blackout curtains protect the toddler's schedule:** Toddlers are far more light-sensitive than newborns in the first months, so protecting their light environment helps the [toddler's nap schedule hold steady](/blog/newborn-toddler-nap-schedule) even as the newborn's nighttime patterns disrupt the room.
:::

section-transition|3. Managing the Transition & Nightlights
badge:🌙 Nighttime Protocol
**Expect initial toddler night wakings:** Some disruption during the adjustment period is normal. Have a consistent plan ready that isn't "give up and let them into your bed every time" — a brief, boring resettling routine rather than an engaging one tends to shorten the adjustment window. **Nightlight placement:** A toddler's nightlight needs and a newborn's safe sleep needs aren't in conflict, but check your specific products. A dim, warm-toned nightlight is generally fine in a shared room; just confirm it's not positioned to shine directly into the bassinet or crib, and that nothing battery-operated or with cords is within reach of either sleep space.

:::checklist
✅ Shared Room Setup Action Checklist
- **Position Sleep Spaces:** Place the newborn closest to the bedroom door and the toddler furthest away.
- **Deploy Dual Sound Machines:** Set up individual white noise units near each sleep surface.
- **Install Full Blackout Shades:** Protect toddler nap windows from ambient light during daytime newborn changes.
- **Safety & Cord Check:** Verify nightlight cords, monitors, and battery boxes are completely out of reach.
- **Set Exit Criteria:** If both kids sleep worse after several weeks, revisit the setup without guilt — room-sharing is a choice, not a mandate.
:::

## When it's genuinely not working

If after several weeks both kids are consistently sleeping worse in a shared room than either did separately, it's reasonable to revisit the arrangement — room-sharing siblings is a logistics choice, not a developmental requirement, and reversing course isn't a failure if the data (consistently worse sleep for one or both kids) says it's not working for your family.
