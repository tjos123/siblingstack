The first time you started solids, it was a whole event — a dedicated high chair moment, a camera out, purees you made yourself in batches. The second time, you're doing it while still nursing a toddler on demand, and the whole thing has to fit into gaps that didn't exist the first time around.

Here's what actually made this workable the second time.

## Make sure baby's actually ready first

It's easy to let the calendar drive this the second time around, since you already know "6 months" as a number. But readiness is about development, not just the date on the calendar. Before you start, your infant should show most of these:

- Can sit upright with minimal support and hold their head steady through a meal
- Has lost the strong tongue-thrust reflex (food doesn't get pushed straight back out)
- Shows real interest in food — watching you eat, reaching for your plate
- Can bring an object to their mouth on their own

If they're not quite there at 6 months, waiting a couple of weeks costs nothing. Starting before these signs show up usually just means more frustration for both of you and doesn't get you to "real eating" any faster.

## The real conflict isn't food, it's your lap

The logistical problem with starting solids on baby two usually isn't the food itself — it's that your toddler wants to nurse at the exact moments your infant needs to be in a [high chair](/gear/high-chair-roundup) getting spoon-fed, and you only have one lap and one set of hands. The fix isn't a feeding schedule overhaul. It's sequencing the two things so they don't compete for the same five minutes.

## Anchor solids to a toddler-occupied window

Instead of picking a "good" time for solids in isolation, pick a time when your toddler is already independently occupied — not because you're ignoring them, but because a spoon-feeding session needs two free hands and undivided attention on the infant, at least at the start.

Good windows to test:
- Right after a toddler's own meal, while they're still in their seat finishing up
- During a toddler's [independent play stretch](/blog/feeding-newborn-entertaining-toddler-activities) (even 10 minutes counts)
- Right after a [toddler nap](/blog/newborn-toddler-nap-schedule) starts, if your infant's hunger cues line up close enough

You're not looking for a perfect schedule. You're looking for a 10-minute pocket that repeats often enough to become a habit.

## What order to nurse in, and why your milk isn't "confused"

If both kids want to nurse around the same window, most lactation guidance points the same direction: feed the infant first, since their nutritional needs are more urgent and less flexible than a toddler's — a toddler is getting the bulk of their calories from food and milk from other sources by now, so a short wait doesn't cost them anything nutritionally. Some parents alternate which side each child starts on, or dedicate one side per child; either works, it's a personal-comfort choice more than a medical one.

It's also worth knowing your supply isn't "confused" by feeding two ages at once — plenty of parents tandem nurse a toddler and a newborn through the newborn's colostrum stage and beyond without issue. What does sometimes happen is a faster letdown than your infant is used to, since you're now making milk for two; if that's causing sputtering or frustration at the breast, offering the toddler's side first to trigger letdown before switching to the infant can smooth that out.

## Nursing the toddler doesn't have to stop — it has to move

If your toddler is still nursing for comfort as much as nutrition, that need doesn't disappear just because a new eater showed up. What changes is *when*. Many parents find it easier to shift toddler nursing sessions to before or after the infant's solids window rather than trying to do both at once — nurse the toddler right before you start the infant's solids, so the toddler's need is met going in, rather than competing with it mid-spoon.

## One spoon, two purposes

You don't need a separate set of purees for a while. Whatever you're already making or buying for the infant, your toddler can usually eat too, at a coarser texture or bigger portion — which means fewer separate cooking sessions, not more. A shared lunch of mashed sweet potato and soft-cooked pasta works at both a 7-month and a 2-year-old's table, just plated differently.

Lead with iron-rich foods specifically, not just fruit and veg purees — pediatric guidance is fairly consistent that infants need an outside source of iron starting around 6 months, especially breastfed ones. Iron-fortified infant cereal, mashed beans or lentils, and well-cooked pureed meat all work, and all scale up in texture for your toddler's plate the same way the fruits and vegetables do.

## Allergens: introduce early, not late, and one at a time

This is the one piece of starting-solids advice that's actually changed in the last decade, and it's worth getting right the second time even if you delayed allergens with your first: current pediatric guidance recommends introducing common allergens — peanut, egg, dairy, wheat, tree nuts, soy, fish — early, around 6 months, rather than holding off. Delaying doesn't prevent allergies and may make them more likely.

The practical version with a toddler underfoot: introduce one new allergen at a time, in an age-appropriate form (thinned peanut butter mixed into puree, well-cooked scrambled egg), and watch for a reaction over the next couple of hours before introducing the next one. If your infant has severe eczema or a diagnosed food allergy already, loop in your pediatrician before starting allergens rather than doing this solo — that's the one case where the general "start early" advice gets adjusted case by case.

## Know the difference between gagging and choking

This matters more with a toddler in the room, because gagging looks and sounds alarming and a toddler reacting to it can make it feel like more of an emergency than it is. Gagging is loud, active, and the baby's face usually reddens as they push the food forward themselves — it's a normal, protective reflex as they learn to manage new textures, not a sign something's wrong. Choking is different and needs your attention immediately: it's silent or nearly silent, the baby can't cry or cough, and their color changes. Feed your infant upright, buckled into the high chair, with you watching directly rather than half-watching while managing the toddler — this is the one window in the day that genuinely can't be done on autopilot.

## Watch for the actual conflict signal, not the calendar

The clearest sign your solids timing needs adjusting isn't a missed "schedule" — it's your toddler acting out specifically during the infant's feeding window. That's usually not jealousy about solids in the abstract. It's a toddler registering that your [attention](/blog/one-on-one-time-each-kid-close-in-age) just left them at a predictable time each day. Two fixes that work better than pushing through it:

- Give the toddler a small "job" during solids — holding a napkin, "helping" with the spoon between bites — so they're included instead of sidelined
- If the timing keeps producing meltdowns, move the window rather than the toddler's routine; the solids schedule is far more flexible than nap or nurse timing

## If your toddler suddenly wants to nurse more, not less

Some toddlers respond to a new sibling's arrival by asking to nurse more often, not less — including toddlers who'd mostly weaned themselves before the birth. This is common enough that lactation resources treat it as a normal reaction rather than a [regression to worry about](/blog/sibling-jealousy-close-age-gap). You get to decide how much of that you want to accommodate; there's no rule that says you have to say yes to every request, and there's no rule that says you have to cut it off either. If the extra nursing is making solids logistics harder, that's a reasonable thing to gently limit — offering a snack or a hug as a substitute in the moment — without it meaning anything larger about the relationship.

## What to actually let go of

- Purees don't need to be homemade to count. Store-bought pouches are not a downgrade when your hands are already full with a toddler.
- Some meals will just be infant-solids-only with the toddler nowhere near the table. That's fine — not every meal needs to be a shared moment.
- The "clean plate" instinct from the first kid doesn't apply here. Baby two eating less, messier, or more erratically than baby one did at the same age isn't a red flag — it's just a different kid with a toddler sibling creating a louder, busier eating environment.
- Feeling "touched out" between nursing a toddler and spoon-feeding an infant is a real and common feeling, not a sign you're doing something wrong. A few minutes without anyone touching you, whenever you can grab it, isn't selfish — it's what lets you keep doing both jobs well.

## Common questions

**Do I need to introduce allergens differently with a toddler around?**
No — the guidance (early, one at a time, watch for a reaction) is the same regardless of who else is in the house. The only adjustment is logistical: do allergen introductions during a window when you can actually watch your infant closely, not while also chasing a toddler.

**Will nursing my toddler less as they eat more solids affect my milk supply for the baby?**
Not typically. Your body responds mainly to the infant's demand at the breast; a toddler nursing less (because they're eating more table food) doesn't reduce what's available for your newborn.

**Is it normal for my toddler to regress and want more milk after the baby starts solids?**
Yes. It's a common reaction to the shift in attention that solids introduces, not a sign of a real setback, and it typically settles within a few weeks as the new routine becomes familiar.
