Finding out you're pregnant again while still breastfeeding raises a question that doesn't have one universal answer: do you keep going, start weaning now, or let it resolve on its own. All three are reasonable paths, and which one fits depends on things specific to you that a general article can't tell you — which is really the main point of this piece.

## The first thing to know: continuing is generally considered safe for most pregnancies

For most healthy, low-risk pregnancies, continuing to breastfeed while pregnant is generally considered medically safe, and many people do it without complication through to delivery and beyond, into [tandem nursing once the new baby arrives](/blog/feeding-schedule-two-different-ages). That said, "most" isn't "all" — certain situations (a history of preterm labor, certain pregnancy complications, or specific medical guidance for your individual case) can change that answer, which is exactly why this is a conversation for your OB or midwife specifically, not something to decide from general information alone.

## What tends to change physically, regardless of your decision

**Nursing during pregnancy is commonly described as more uncomfortable than before**, often due to increased hormone-driven nipple sensitivity. This alone leads some people to choose to wean, not because anything is wrong, but simply because it stops being comfortable.

**Milk supply often decreases during pregnancy**, sometimes substantially, as your body's hormones shift toward supporting the new pregnancy. This is a normal physiological response, not a sign you're doing anything wrong, and it sometimes leads an older nursling to [self-wean as the supply and taste of milk changes](/blog/sibling-jealousy-close-age-gap), independent of any decision you make.

**Fatigue compounds in a way that's hard to overstate.** Pregnancy fatigue and the physical demands of nursing a toddler are each tiring on their own; together, they're genuinely more draining than either alone, and it's worth planning for [more rest and support](/blog/solo-bedtime-2-under-2-alone) than either situation would require by itself. If you're still in the early postpartum stretch of the last pregnancy while now growing the next one, [postpartum recovery when you're pregnant again right away](/blog/postpartum-recovery-pregnant-again) covers more of what's different about that specific combination.

## If you're deciding whether to wean now

This is a genuinely personal decision, and there's no version of "what's right" that doesn't depend on specifics only you and your provider know — your milk supply, your toddler's relationship with nursing, any pregnancy complications, your own physical and emotional capacity. A few things worth knowing as you think it through:

**Abrupt weaning is generally harder on both of you than gradual weaning**, if you have the flexibility to choose. A slower transition tends to be more comfortable physically (reducing risk of engorgement) and [emotionally easier for a toddler](/blog/one-on-one-time-each-kid-close-in-age) who's used to nursing as part of their routine, not just their nutrition.

**A lactation consultant can help regardless of which direction you're headed** — whether you want support continuing through pregnancy and into tandem nursing, or support weaning gently. This is specifically their area of expertise, and a single consultation can answer questions a general article never could, tailored to your actual situation.

**There's no deadline you're failing to meet.** Whether you nursed for two months or two years, whether you wean before the new baby or nurse both afterward, none of these timelines are a measure of anything except what worked for your family.

## If you decide to continue, or to tandem nurse after the new baby arrives

Many people do this successfully, and resources specifically about tandem nursing (there are several well-regarded books and lactation-consultant-written guides on the topic) go into far more practical detail than fits here — worth seeking out specifically if this is the direction you're headed, since the logistics (positioning two kids of very different sizes, managing supply for both) are genuinely their own topic.

## The actual bottom line

Whatever you decide, it's worth having an explicit conversation with your OB or midwife about your specific situation, and a lactation consultant if you want hands-on support either continuing or weaning. General guidance can tell you what's commonly true; it can't tell you what's right for your specific pregnancy and your specific toddler.
