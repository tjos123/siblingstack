Two "Montessori" subscription boxes, one playroom holding a 10-month-old and a newborn, and a family that had exactly one toy budget. That was the setup for our head-to-head test of **Hoppi Box** and **Monti Kids** — the two direct-style developmental toy subscriptions, distinct enough from Lovevery's approach to be their own category, and popular enough that "which one is better?" is one of the most-searched questions in the two-under-two parenting corners of the internet. We ran both boxes simultaneously for six months, played everything, tracked what got used, what got ignored, what survived a newborn's gumming and a 10-month-old's investigations, and what the two subscription philosophies really buy you. Here's the full comparison, from shipping to resale.

## What Each Box Actually Is

Before the head-to-head, thirty seconds of context, because these two brands sound interchangeable and are not.

**Hoppi Box** is a stage-based Montessori toy subscription built around an age-specific delivery cadence of skill-boosting, open-ended toys, with a strong educational-play focus. This was a younger-kid bent in our experience — the infant stages lean toward sensory, grasping, and cause-and-effect materials, and the boxes arrive with materials designed to be introduced at a child's exact readiness window.

**Monti Kids** is a more systematized Montessori curriculum subscription: each box is tied to an online parent-education program and a defined developmental level, with the toys functioning as the physical materials for a structured home-learning plan. It's the closest thing in the toy-subscription world to a DIY Montessori home curriculum, where the weekly login, the milestone guide, and the box arrive together as a single system.

Both share the same philosophical lineage — Maria Montessori's "a child's environment is the curriculum" — but one sells you toys, and the other sells you a program with the toys embedded in it. That distinction drove almost every worthwhile difference in our test.

The honest scorecard isn't a winner by knockout — it's a winner by _use case_. If you want the developmental toy experience with minimal parent overhead, Hoppi Box delivers cleanly and sits at the friendlier price point. If you're the parent who wants a program to follow — the kind who reads every parenting manual and wants the "how" explained — Monti Kids justifies its premium with the course layer. What neither box does well for a two-under-two household is cover two children simultaneously; both are per-child a commitment, and neither's program scales down cleanly when the second baby arrives mid-subscription.

## Two Kids, One Subscription: The 10-Month-Old and the Newborn

This is the test nobody else runs, because most subscription-box reviewers have one kid. Our playroom had a 10-month-old firmly in the grasping-and-object-permanence phase and a newborn freshly born. Here's what that did to the two boxes.

**Hoppi Box at a 10-month-old and newborn together:** The infant-stage Hoppi materials (grasping toys, sensory discs, simple cause-and-effect mechanisms) were perfect for the 10-month-old and, six to eight months later, became equally perfect for the newborn as she reached the same readiness window. The brand's stage timing is genuinely well-tuned; we got two-for-one value out of the infant boxes because the pass-down window lined up — same materials, two kids, one purchase. That's the sibling-math sweet spot, exactly the [kind of pass-down leverage the wider subscription math](https://www.sibling-stack.com/blog/lovevery-subscription-worth-it-two-kids-under-two) depends on. The only friction: the 10-month-old and the newborn were _almost_ in the same stage band, which meant one contested spat for the best toy and a second-copy decision we had to make deliberately rather than reflexively.

**Monti Kids at a 10-month-old and newborn together:** Monti Kids' structured levels meant each child existed on a separate curriculum track, and the newborn's track barely overlapped the 10-month-old's. That was fine for order, but it multiplied the parent load: two online courses, two milestone calendars, two material sets to keep organized. The boxes themselves were excellent — high-quality wood, watchable materials, genuine Montessori pedagogy — but the _system_ overhead doubled in a two-sibling household in a way that made the subscription's premium price feel like a premium on homework. For a single child, that homework is the product's best feature. For two kids nine months apart, it's the part that eroded our enthusiasm fastest.

## Durability and the Baby-Proofing Test

Every toy in a two-under-two household needs to survive two very different threat models: a newborn's mouth (smooth edges, no small breakoffs, non-toxic finishes) and a 10-month-old's drop-testing, banging, and standing-on. Both boxes did well on the mouth test — no hazardous failures, no peeling finishes, nothing that worried a pediatrician's-eye view. On the toddler-abuse test, the divergence was subtle but real.

- Hoppi Box's materials are thoughtfully minimal — fewer pieces, simpler mechanisms — which makes them harder to break by design. Six months in, nothing had failed.
- Monti Kids' materials are richer and more numerous per level, which means more small parts to lose, more individual pieces to track, and a couple of loose bits that needed regular re-finding. Nothing broke convincingly, but the maintenance load sat noticeably higher in a house where pieces walk away in a pocket.

For a family juggling two kids under two, the practical takeaway isn't "one brand is sturdier than the other" — it's that the _denser_ system (Monti Kids) pushes more of the organizational load onto you, and that load is exactly what two-under-two parents have the least of. The hygiene angle matters here too: when small pieces roam a shared play space, the [two-kid cleaning-and-sanitizing protocol](https://www.sibling-stack.com/blog/clean-sanitize-used-lovevery-toys-sibling-hygiene-guide) becomes part of the daily routine, and simpler kilts (borrowing Hoppi's approach) are simply faster to cycle through the wash-and-wipe line.

## The Parent Program Question

The single biggest philosophical difference between the two boxes is the parent component, and it split our household almost exactly down the middle. One parent loved Monti Kids' course layer — the "why this material, why this order, what to watch for" guidance is genuinely useful for a first timer, and the milestone tracking kept the bigger developmental picture front-of-mind. The other parent found the program layer to be the exact opposite of what a subscription should be: more homework at the end of a day already full of two tiny humans, and a reason to feel guilty when the week's course hadn't been watched.

The fair synthesis: Monti Kids is the better _education_ purchase and Hoppi Box is the better _toy_ purchase. If you already know Montessori principles, or if you've already absorbed the "watch, don't interfere, let the child work" core once, the program layer of Monti Kids adds less incremental value, and the toy-forward Hoppi box gives you more play-per-dollar. If you're brand-new to Montessori and want structure, the program's premium is easier to justify. Neither answer is wrong; they just serve different families.

## Price, Resale, and the Two-Under-Two Budget View

Both boxes sit in the premium tier of toy subscriptions, and both pitch "quality materials as long-term value." In a two-child household, that pitch becomes an actual test — because everything you buy is going to be played with by two kids and then sold or passed on.

- **Hoppi Box** prices competitively among stage subscriptions, and its simplicity means every dollar of box cost converts into playable materials rather than curriculum infrastructure. Resale for the mounted, simple wood pieces has been decent on the usual marketplaces.
- **Monti Kids** prices at a premium that includes the online program — you're paying for education, not just materials. The physical materials hold strong resale value (the wood quality is visible in hand), but the premium is genuinely tied to the courseware, and a buyer is paying more to enter a system than to own six nice toys.

For our ledger, the deciding line was the forklift question again: a two-under-two household profits most from subscriptions whose value compounds two-child-deep — durability, pass-down geometry, and resale. That favors whichever box your family will _actually_ keep rotating and reselling. Given equal discipline, Hoppi Box's lower entry cost and lower maintenance load made it the better budget system for us, while Monti Kids' program made it the better system for structure. If the honest version of your parenting style wants structure, buy Monti Kids; if it wants toys that do Montessori-aligned work without a course, Hoppi Box earns its place in the playroom.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are Hoppi Box and Monti Kids the same as Lovevery, or genuinely different?**
Genuinely different category. Lovevery sells stage-matched toys with a light parent guide; Hoppi Box and Monti Kids both sit closer to a Montessori teaching system. The practical difference is that Lovevery optimizes for a self-directed toy library, while these two optimize for aligned learning experiences — Hoppi via toys, Monti Kids via an actual curriculum.

**Which one is cheaper over a full year?**
Hoppi Box, clearly. It sits at a friendlier price point and its box cost converts almost entirely into materials. Monti Kids' premium buys the parent-education layer, so you're comparing a toy subscription to an education subscription. The gap narrows only if the program genuinely gets used and resale extracts value well, which is the realistic best case.

**Can one subscription serve two kids under two at once?**
Partially. Hoppi Box's infant materials pass down nearly perfectly because its stage windows are clean — same materials for the younger sibling a few months later. Monti Kids' structured levels technically put each child on a separate track, which doubles the program load. Neither brand is built for simultaneous dual subscription, so a two-under-two household will still manage a second-copy or stagger decision.

**Did the newborn get meaningful use out of either box in the first months?**
Yes, but differently. Hoppi's infant materials served the newborn directly as she reached each readiness window, since they're age-specific by design. Monti Kids' newborn materials are likewise well-designed and took over seamlessly, but the full value arrived only once the reaching-and-grasping stage hit — expect the first month or two to be low-use for a baby who mostly sleeps, nurses, and stares.

**Is the parent program worth it if I already have a toddler?**
Much less so. The program teaches a first-time parent the how-and-why of Montessori guidance. If you've already internalized the core approach with child #1, the marginal value drops steeply, and the toy-forward subscription becomes the better spend of the same dollars.

**Which do you actually recommend for a two-under-two household?**
If the parent driving the research wants a program to follow and has room in the time budget, Monti Kids. For the typical exhausted two-under-two household that wants Montessori-aligned toys doing real work without homework, Hoppi Box delivers more play-per-dollar and far less overhead. Run the budget and the personality test before you commit, and consider starting with a single infant box from each before you annualize.

## The Bottom Line

Six months of side-by-side testing turned the "Hoppi Box vs. Monti Kids" question into a much cleaner decision than the internet's forum threads suggest. They're not substitutes — they're two different products in the same aisle: Hoppi Box sells Montessori-aligned toys tuned to readiness windows, and Monti Kids sells a Montessori program with toys as its materials. In a two-under-two household, the choice is really about what you're optimizing for: lower entry cost and near-perfect pass-down of minimal, durable materials (Hoppi), or a structured curriculum that justifies its premium only if you actually engage with the education layer (Monti Kids). Both brands survived our playroom's abuse and both passed the baby test handily; the decider was the parent load, and the toy-forward box won the tired-parent scorecard. Whichever you pick, buy expecting a two-child lifecycle, and let the pass-down geometry — not the unboxing photos — set your expectations.
