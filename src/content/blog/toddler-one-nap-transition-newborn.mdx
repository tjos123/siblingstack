Dropping to one nap is hard enough on its own — and doing it while a newborn is home adds a layer of chaos. The one-nap transition lands around the same window a newborn arrives, and it's the same overlap problem our [newborn-and-toddler nap scheduling guide](/blog/newborn-toddler-nap-schedule) walks through day by day. The good news: the transition itself doesn't change because there's a baby in the house — what changes is how much bandwidth you have to manage it, so the plan needs to be simpler and more forgiving than a textbook version.

:::section
readiness-signs|When Is Your Toddler Actually Ready?
badge:👶 Typical Age: 14–18 Months
badge:🎯 Focus: Readiness Cues
Most toddlers drop to one nap somewhere between 14 and 18 months, though it varies widely. Watch for these distinct signs before making the leap:
- Consistently fighting or delaying the morning nap.
- The morning nap is shrinking to 30 minutes or less.
- Bedtime is getting pushed later because the afternoon nap is too close to it.
- The toddler seems happy (not overtired) on days the morning nap gets skipped.
:::

:::warning
**Worth Knowing:** A newborn's presence can cause temporary toddler sleep regressions. Look for a consistent 2-week pattern of these signs before committing to the schedule shift — you don't want to prematurely drop a nap your toddler still needs. If your toddler is still napping well twice a day, a newborn's arrival is not, by itself, a reason to rush.
:::

:::section
three-week-plan|The 3-Week Realistic Transition Plan
badge:🗓️ Timeline: 21 Days
badge:⚙️ Method: Gradual Shift
The most sustainable approach is to gradually slide the single nap later rather than abruptly cutting the morning nap. **Week 1 — Shift & Shorten:** Push the morning nap 15–30 minutes later (toward 10:30 or 11:00 AM) and cap it around 45 minutes, offering a short 20-minute catnap in the late afternoon if needed. **Week 2 — Consolidate to Midday:** Combine the two naps into one starting between 11:30 AM and 12:30 PM, with an earlier bedtime (by 30–45 minutes) on days the toddler seems worn out. **Week 3 — Establish the Rhythm:** Settle into a single nap around 12:00–1:00 PM lasting 1.5 to 3 hours, with bedtime shifting back to normal as the toddler adjusts.
:::

:::warning
**Pro Tip:** Don't offer a second nap late in the day (after 3:30–4 PM) even on short-nap days — it tends to push bedtime later rather than helping. Move bedtime earlier instead. Expect some rough days in the middle of the slide (extra fussiness, one blown nap) — that's normal and temporary, not a sign the transition is failing.
:::

newborn-hacks|Managing the Shift with a Newborn in the House
badge:🧠 Strategy: Multi-Child Execution
badge:👶 Newborn Alignment
Two major challenges arise when transitioning naps alongside a baby: quiet-house overlap and low parent bandwidth. The workaround isn't to force perfect consistency — it's to anchor the toddler's nap to something more reliable than the clock, like a ritual, and use these tactical hacks:
- **Pre-Nap Baby Feed:** Feed the baby right before starting the toddler's nap ritual to prevent interruptions mid-book.
- **Babywearing Power Move:** Put the baby in a wrap or carrier during toddler nap setup to keep your hands free.
- **Station Stashing:** Pre-load a feeding station near the toddler's room so you can handle a baby feed without leaving the room.
- **10-Minute Buffer Rule:** If the toddler wakes early, give them 10 minutes of quiet time before intervening — they may resettle on their own.
:::

## Anchor the nap to a ritual, not a rigid clock

Instead of aiming for an exact minute, build a 10-minute pre-nap ritual that stays the same regardless of what the baby is doing: same two books, same phrase ("nap time now"), same white noise. Toddlers respond to the *ritual* as the cue to wind down more than the exact clock time, which gives you flexibility to shift the nap by 20–30 minutes on hard days without derailing it. This also matters if you're [setting up a shared room](/blog/crib-and-bassinet-setup-two-babies-one-room) where a newborn is anchoring in the same space.

## What to do when the baby's feed collides with toddler nap time

This is the single most common flashpoint during the transition, and it's really the [feeding-schedule problem in miniature](/blog/feeding-schedule-two-different-ages). Feed the baby right before starting the toddler's nap ritual, use a carrier for the baby during nap-time books to free your hands for the toddler, and pre-load a nursing/feeding station in or near the toddler's room so you can do both in the same room without leaving.

## Handling the early-wake-up days

During the transition it's common for toddlers to wake early from the single nap (after 45–60 minutes) because their body isn't yet used to sleeping through what used to be two separate cycles. Give it 10 minutes of quiet time before intervening — if they're truly awake and not resettling, treat it as the nap being done for the day and shift bedtime 30–45 minutes earlier to compensate. Don't offer a second nap late in the day even on short-nap days.

## When to pause the transition

If your toddler is consistently miserable, waking at 4–5 AM, or having daily meltdowns that weren't there before, it's fine to pull back to two (shorter) naps for another 2–3 weeks and try again. The one-nap transition is not a one-shot deadline — most toddlers get two or three attempts before it sticks for good.

## FAQ

**Can a newborn's unpredictable schedule permanently delay the one-nap transition?**
No — it can slow the process by a couple of weeks, but toddlers still transition on roughly their own biological timeline regardless of what else is happening in the house.

**Is it normal for the one nap to be too short at first (under an hour)?**
Yes, this is common in the first 1–2 weeks. It usually lengthens to 1.5–3 hours as the toddler's body adjusts to consolidating sleep into one window.

**Should I keep the morning nap on weekends or with grandparents to make things easier?**
It's best to keep the schedule consistent across all days during the transition weeks — inconsistent napping (one nap on weekdays, two on weekends) tends to extend how long the transition takes.
