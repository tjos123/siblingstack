The one-nap transition is already one of the trickiest sleep shifts of toddlerhood — and it has terrible timing when it lands around the same window a newborn arrives (the same overlap problem our [newborn-and-toddler nap scheduling guide](/blog/newborn-toddler-nap-schedule) walks through day by day). The good news: the transition itself doesn't change because there's a baby in the house. What changes is how much bandwidth you have to manage it, so the plan needs to be simpler and more forgiving than a textbook version.

## How to know your toddler is ready

Most toddlers drop to one nap somewhere between 14 and 18 months, though it varies widely. Signs it's time:

- Consistently fighting or delaying the morning nap
- Morning nap is shrinking to 30 minutes or less
- Bedtime is getting pushed later because the afternoon nap is too close to it
- The toddler seems fine (not overtired) on days the morning nap gets skipped

If your toddler is still napping well twice a day and isn't showing these signs, it's usually worth waiting — a newborn's arrival is not, by itself, a reason to rush the transition.

## The core method: slide, don't cut

The most sustainable approach is to gradually push the single nap later, rather than abruptly cutting the morning nap and hoping the toddler survives until 1 PM.

**A typical 2–3 week slide:**
- **Week 1:** Push wake-up-and-out-of-bed time 15–20 minutes later if possible, and delay the morning nap by 30 minutes.
- **Week 2:** Combine the two naps into one nap starting between 11:30 AM and 12:30 PM, offering an earlier bedtime (by 30–45 minutes) on days the toddler seems worn out.
- **Week 3:** Settle into a single nap around 12–1 PM, lasting 1.5–3 hours, with bedtime shifting back to normal as the toddler adjusts.

Expect some rough days in the middle of the slide — extra fussiness, one blown nap, an early bedtime. That's normal and temporary, not a sign the transition is failing.

If you're not sure where the shifted nap should land each day, the [wake window calculator](/tools/calculator) and [nap sync calculator](/tools/nap-sync-calculator) do the timing math for you — the nap sync version is especially useful here, since sliding one kid's nap around a newborn's unpredictable day is exactly its job.

## Why this is harder with a newborn in the house

Two specific things make the timing brutal:

1. **The overlap problem.** A newborn's daytime naps are frequent and short, meaning there's rarely a "quiet house" window. During the toddler's transition weeks, the toddler is more likely to skip or shorten naps — and there's no built-in recovery time because the baby also needs you.
2. **You have less capacity to hold a firm, consistent routine.** Nap transitions go more smoothly with consistent timing, and consistency is exactly what a newborn's unpredictable schedule disrupts.

The workaround isn't to force perfect consistency — it's to anchor the toddler's nap to something more reliable than the clock, like a location or ritual. (If you're also setting up a shared room right now, our [crib and bassinet setup guide](/blog/crib-and-bassinet-setup-two-babies-one-room) covers anchoring a toddler's nap when a newborn is in the same space.)

## Anchor the nap to a ritual, not a rigid clock

Instead of aiming for an exact minute, build a 10-minute pre-nap ritual that stays the same regardless of what the baby is doing: same two books, same phrase ("nap time now"), same white noise. Toddlers respond to the *ritual* as the cue to wind down more than the exact clock time, which gives you flexibility to shift the nap by 20–30 minutes on hard days without derailing it.

## What to do when the baby's feed collides with toddler nap time

This is the single most common flashpoint during the transition, and it's really the feeding-schedule problem in miniature — see [managing a feeding schedule for two different ages](/blog/feeding-schedule-two-different-ages) for the broader version. A few field-tested options:

- **Feed the baby right before starting the toddler's nap ritual**, so you're not interrupted mid-book.
- **Use a carrier or wrap** for the baby during the nap-time books, freeing your hands for the toddler.
- **Pre-load a nursing/feeding station** in or near the toddler's room, so if the baby needs a feed exactly at nap time, you can do both in the same room without leaving the toddler.

## Handling the early-wake-up days

During the transition, it's common for toddlers to wake early from the single nap (after 45–60 minutes) because their body isn't yet used to sleeping through what used to be two separate sleep cycles. If this happens:

- Give it 10 minutes of quiet time before intervening — some toddlers resettle on their own.
- If they're truly awake and not resettling, treat it as the nap being done for the day, and shift bedtime 30–45 minutes earlier to compensate.
- Don't offer a second nap late in the day (after 3:30–4 PM) even on short-nap days — it tends to push bedtime later, not earlier.

## A sample "in transition" daily schedule

- **7:00 AM** – Wake up
- **9:30 AM** – Optional short "bridge" nap in the car or stroller only if the toddler is clearly struggling (skip once fully transitioned)
- **12:00–12:15 PM** – Lunch, then nap ritual begins
- **12:30–2:30 PM** – Single nap
- **7:00–7:15 PM** – Bedtime (earlier than usual during transition weeks)

## When to pause the transition

If your toddler is consistently miserable, waking up at 4–5 AM, or having daily meltdowns that weren't there before, it's fine to pull back to two (shorter) naps for another 2–3 weeks and try again. The one-nap transition is not a one-shot deadline — most toddlers get two or three attempts at it before it sticks for good.

## FAQ

**Can a newborn's unpredictable schedule permanently delay the one-nap transition?**
No — it can slow the process by a couple of weeks, but toddlers will still transition on roughly their own biological timeline regardless of what else is happening in the house.

**Is it normal for the one nap to be too short at first (under an hour)?**
Yes, this is common in the first 1–2 weeks. It usually lengthens to 1.5–3 hours as the toddler's body adjusts to consolidating sleep into one window.

**Should I keep the morning nap on weekends or with grandparents to make things easier?**
It's best to keep the schedule consistent across all days during the transition weeks — inconsistent napping (one nap on weekdays, two on weekends) tends to extend how long the transition takes.
