## Why This Testing Matters More Than Ingredient Lists

Every materials comparison in this cluster so far — the topsheet-versus-topsheet comparisons in Articles 2, 3, 11, and 12 — has relied on what brands themselves publish about their own formulations, cross-referenced against named third-party certifications like OEKO-TEX Standard 100. That's a reasonable methodology as far as it goes, but it has a specific blind spot: **a certification is only as good as what it actually tests for**, and this section of this article's research uncovered a real, documented gap in exactly the certification this cluster has leaned on most heavily for Kudos and Dyper specifically.

The testing summarized in this article was conducted by an EPA-certified laboratory on behalf of Mamavation, a consumer-testing organization, in partnership with Environmental Health News, with scientific review from four named, credentialed toxicologists: Terrence Collins (Director of the Institute for Green Sciences at Carnegie Mellon University), Linda Birnbaum (former Director of the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences and National Toxicology Program), Pete Myers (Chief Scientist at Environmental Health Sciences), and Scott Belcher (Associate Professor at the Center for Environmental & Health Effects of PFAS at North Carolina State University). This is independent, credentialed, methodologically documented testing — not a marketing claim from a competing brand, and not an anonymous internet allegation.

## The Testing Methodology, Briefly

The lab tested for organic fluorine — a chemical marker present in all PFAS compounds — using Determination of Total Fluorine by Oxygen Flask Combustion and Ion-Selective Electrode, with results above a 10 parts-per-million (ppm) detection threshold flagged as indications of PFAS. Both the inside (skin-contact) and outside surfaces of each diaper were tested separately. Sixty-five diaper products from 40 brands were tested between February and August 2023, purchased directly by Mamavation or donated by community members, with results published starting in November 2023 and updated through August 2024. Mamavation's own stated limitation is worth repeating rather than glossing over: this was a "spot-check" of the industry, not an exhaustive or ongoing audit, and the organization explicitly states it "cannot make predictions about brands and products that we have not tested" — including, this article would add, products manufactured or reformulated after the testing window closed.

## The OEKO-TEX Certification Gap: Why This Wasn't Caught

This is the single most important explanatory finding in this article, because it directly affects how this cluster's readers should weigh OEKO-TEX certification going forward — a credential cited as a positive trust signal for both Kudos and Dyper throughout this series.

OEKO-TEX Standard 100 does test for PFAS in some of its certification tiers, but the specific method it mandates — Extractable Organic Fluorine (EOF) testing — has a documented technical blind spot: **it does not extract PFAS polymers, including polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), the same family of chemical as the coating on non-stick cookware.** Mamavation's reporting states plainly that this is not a hypothetical concern: "We found two perfect examples of this possibility in our testing when we found an indication of PFAS in diapers with OEKO-TEX certification" — and Kudos is one of the two brands referenced. Total organic fluorine testing, the method Mamavation's lab used, captures PTFE and other fluoropolymers that EOF testing misses, which is precisely why a product can be OEKO-TEX certified and still test positive under a more comprehensive testing method.

Terrence Collins of Carnegie Mellon, reviewing the study, added a second, independent critique of the same certification: even where OEKO-TEX's EOF method does apply, its Class 1 standard (the tier for baby products) currently allows up to 10 ppm of Extractable Organic Fluorine — a threshold Collins specifically called "too much, way too much" relative to current drinking-water PFAS safety standards. **This means OEKO-TEX certification, as currently structured, cannot be read as a PFAS-free guarantee even on its own terms, independent of the PTFE testing gap.** This is a direct, credentialed, scientifically-sourced correction to how this cluster has previously characterized that certification.

**California's regulatory response is directly relevant here too.** AB 652, effective July 1, 2023, prohibits intentionally added PFAS above specific thresholds in children's products sold in California, explicitly using _total_ organic fluorine as the compliance standard — not extractable organic fluorine, precisely because the state recognized that EOF testing misses PTFE. A product could plausibly be OEKO-TEX certified and still fail to meet California's total-organic-fluorine-based standard, a regulatory tension Mamavation's reporting flags directly and this article is relaying accurately rather than independently verifying against California enforcement action, since no such enforcement action against any brand in this cluster was identified in this research.

## The Coterie Lawsuit: A Precise Legal Timeline

The Coterie vs. Kudos comparison raised concerns about Coterie's plastic (polypropylene) topsheet, contrasting it against Kudos's cotton topsheet. This article's PFAS findings complicate that narrative in an important way, and there's a real legal history attached that deserves precise handling rather than a vague mention.

**May 2024:** A proposed class action, _Saedi et al. v. Coterie Baby, Inc._ (Case No. 24-cv-3893, U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York), was filed, alleging Coterie deceptively marketed its diapers as "PFAS-free" or "below detectable levels" for perfluorinated compounds despite the products actually containing PFAS. The complaint reportedly argued that manufacturing a genuinely 100% PFAS-free diaper is essentially impossible given how ubiquitous PFAS chemicals have become in the environment and supply chain generally.

**August 2024:** Mamavation independently sent Coterie Size One diapers back to its lab for an expanded test — 40 specific PFAS analytes, tested down to parts-per-trillion sensitivity, a substantially more rigorous test than the organic-fluorine screening used in the original study. The result: non-detect across the board.

**October 4, 2024:** A federal judge dismissed the lawsuit. Critically, the dismissal was on **standing grounds** — the court found lead plaintiff Roz Saedi had not adequately alleged that the _specific diapers she personally purchased_ actually contained PFAS, which is a pleading defect, not a substantive ruling on whether Coterie's products do or don't contain PFAS in general. This distinction matters and this article isn't going to blur it: a dismissal for lack of standing doesn't vindicate a company's underlying product claims, it means this particular plaintiff didn't plead her individual case adequately.

**Ongoing tracking discrepancy worth flagging:** Truth in Advertising (TINA.org), a consumer-advocacy litigation tracker, continued to list the case status as "Pending" across multiple pages of its site reviewed for this research, which doesn't fully reconcile with the October 2024 dismissal reported by Bloomberg Law and PFAS Central. This could reflect an amended complaint, an appeal, or simply a tracker page that hadn't been updated at the time of this research — this article could not resolve that discrepancy definitively and is reporting it as an open question rather than guessing at an explanation.

**Putting the legal and lab findings together:** Coterie was sued over PFAS claims, defeated that suit on procedural grounds (not a substantive vindication), and separately, through Mamavation's own independent lab testing — both the initial study and a more rigorous August 2024 follow-up — tested clean for PFAS. Two different forms of scrutiny, one legal and one scientific, and neither one found confirmed PFAS contamination in Coterie's product, even though the legal claim was never substantively resolved either way. This is a meaningfully different, more reassuring picture on the specific PFAS question than the plastic-topsheet finding in The Coterie vs. Kudos comparison might suggest on its own — the two findings (polypropylene topsheet, clean PFAS testing) aren't in conflict; a synthetic topsheet material and PFAS treatment are separate manufacturing choices, and Coterie's, per available evidence, doesn't appear to involve the latter.

## Why PFAS Exposure Specifically Concerns Infants

This article has referenced PFAS as a health concern throughout without detailing why, and given this cluster's consistent practice of grounding claims rather than asserting them, that deserves its own treatment here. PFAS are a large class of synthetic chemicals used for decades for their water-, oil-, and stain-resistant properties — the same basic chemistry family behind non-stick cookware coatings and water-repellent fabric treatments. Linda Birnbaum, the former NIEHS/NTP director who reviewed the study this article summarizes, stated the concern plainly: "They accumulate inside the body and are close to impossible to get rid of. We already know that exposure to these chemicals can cause problems in pregnant women, but the effects on babies at this young age are mostly unknown. But just because we don't know all the health impacts doesn't mean they are safe."

The broader toxicological literature referenced in Mamavation's reporting associates PFAS exposure with a range of effects, summarized here as a general risk profile rather than a claim about any individual product or child: reduced immune function and vaccine response, disrupted growth and behavioral development in infants and children, endocrine and thyroid disruption, increased cholesterol, and — in longer-term population studies — associations with certain cancers and reduced fertility. Scott Belcher of NC State's PFAS research center specifically flagged that total organic fluorine testing, the method used in this study, is a legitimate way to "spot-check" for PFAS broadly, including fluoropolymers like PTFE that narrower single-compound tests would miss entirely.

**Why this matters more for a diaper specifically than many other PFAS-adjacent products**: the diaper area is in prolonged, direct, repeated contact with an infant's skin for the first two to three years of life, during a developmental window when — per Birnbaum's own caution above — the specific health effects of this kind of exposure in very young children remain under-researched rather than well-characterized. This isn't a basis for alarm calibrated to any specific outcome, since the research base on this specific exposure pathway in infants is, by the toxicologists' own account, incomplete. It is a basis for taking independent verification seriously rather than relying solely on a brand's own "non-toxic" or "clean" marketing language — which is the entire premise this article is built on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Did Kudos diapers test positive for PFAS?**
Yes — three of four samples tested by an EPA-certified lab on behalf of Mamavation showed organic fluorine (a PFAS marker) between 16 and 53 ppm, above the 10 ppm detection threshold. One sample (Size 5) tested non-detect.

**Does Kudos's OEKO-TEX certification mean it's PFAS-free?**
No. OEKO-TEX Standard 100's PFAS testing method (Extractable Organic Fluorine) doesn't extract PFAS polymers like PTFE, meaning a product can be certified and still contain PFAS that the certification's own testing wouldn't catch — which is exactly what this testing found for Kudos.

**Did Coterie test positive for PFAS?**
No — Coterie tested non-detect in the original study and again in an expanded, more sensitive 40-analyte test conducted in August 2024, despite having a synthetic (polypropylene) topsheet, per The Coterie vs. Kudos comparison.

**Was Coterie sued over PFAS claims?**
Yes — a proposed class action filed in May 2024 alleged deceptive PFAS-free marketing. It was dismissed in October 2024 on standing grounds (the plaintiff didn't adequately allege her specific diapers contained PFAS), which is a procedural outcome, not a substantive finding on the underlying PFAS question.

**Did Hello Bello, Honest, Dyper, or Abby & Finn test positive for PFAS?**
No — all four tested non-detect for organic fluorine on both the inside and outside of the samples tested.

**Is this testing current?**
No — testing was conducted between February 2023 and August 2024, with results published and updated through April 2024 (and the Coterie retest in August 2024). Formulations can change without notice, and this article cannot confirm whether any brand's current product matches what was tested.

**Does this affect the 2026 Kudos manufacturing situation covered in The Kudos 2026 manufacturing investigation?**
Not directly — this testing predates the reported 2026 redesign. Whether the current, redesigned Kudos product has the same PFAS profile as the pre-2026 product tested here is unconfirmed in either direction.

**What is organic fluorine, and why is it used as a PFAS marker?**
All PFAS chemicals are carbon-based compounds containing fluorine. Testing for total organic fluorine captures the presence of PFAS broadly, including specific compounds that narrower, single-chemical tests might miss — though it can also occasionally capture other fluorine-containing substances unrelated to PFAS, a limitation of the method worth knowing about.

**Should I stop using Kudos based on this article?**
This article documents lab results and lets you weigh them; it's not making that decision for you. Given the explicit caveat that this data predates 2026 product changes, a reasonable next step for a current Kudos household concerned about this finding is contacting the company directly for current testing information, rather than assuming this 2023–2024 data describes today's product with certainty in either direction.

This specific study focused on organic fluorine as a PFAS marker. Mamavation's broader reporting, reviewed for this research, also discusses other categories of concern across the diaper industry generally — chlorine bleaching methods, VOCs, undisclosed fragrances, phthalates, and organotin compounds — though brand-specific results for those categories, beyond what's already documented elsewhere in this cluster, were outside this article's scope.

**Is any brand in this cluster completely free of any chemical of concern?**
No brand tested achieved a completely clean bill across every category Mamavation's broader reporting examines — even brands with non-detect PFAS results were flagged for other considerations, like ECF (rather than TCF) chlorine processing for Hello Bello, Honest, and Dyper.
