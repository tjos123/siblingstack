There's a specific kind of dread that comes with hearing your toddler's footsteps heading toward the nursery right as the baby *finally* went down. This is one of the most common friction points in a 2-under-2 household, and it's rarely about the toddler being deliberately disruptive — it's almost always about curiosity, boredom, or wanting your attention back.

The fix isn't one trick; it's a combination of physical setup, timing, and a consistent script the toddler can predict. (If the toddler's own nap schedule is also shifting around right now — which often coincides with a newborn arriving — see our [toddler one-nap transition guide](/blog/toddler-one-nap-transition-newborn), which covers the same fragile-nap-window problem from the other side.)

## Why toddlers do this

Before troubleshooting, it helps to know what's actually driving the behavior:

- **Curiosity** — the baby is new, interesting, and makes sounds; toddlers are drawn to investigate.
- **Attention-seeking** — if the baby napping means you're suddenly less available, some toddlers learn that "checking on the baby" gets a fast reaction from you.
- **Genuine affection** — many toddlers want to hug, kiss, or talk to the baby, without understanding why that's disruptive during sleep.
- **Boredom** — an unsupervised or under-stimulated toddler will wander, and the nursery is often the most interesting room in the house.

Nearly all of these have workable, non-punitive solutions.

## 1. Separate the spaces during baby's sleep windows

If your layout allows it, make the baby's nap space physically harder for the toddler to wander into unsupervised — a closed door, a gate at the nursery doorway, or simply napping the baby in a room the toddler doesn't play in. This removes the opportunity rather than relying on the toddler to remember a rule mid-play.

If the two share a room, stagger nap times where possible, and use white noise in the shared room to mask normal toddler play sounds so they're less likely to disturb a lightly-sleeping baby. Our [shared-room setup guide](/blog/crib-and-bassinet-setup-two-babies-one-room) covers the layout and dual-sound-machine setup in detail.

## 2. Give the toddler a job during baby's naps

Boredom-driven wandering usually drops sharply when the toddler has something engaging to do the moment the baby goes down. A rotating "quiet time basket" that only comes out during baby's naps — a few toys, a coloring book, a sticker sheet — signals "this is nap time for baby, and also my special activity time," which reframes the baby's nap as a positive event for the toddler instead of a loss of attention. The technique is the same "give the toddler a real job" move used at [solo bedtime](/blog/solo-bedtime-2-under-2-alone) and during [newborn feeds](/blog/feeding-newborn-entertaining-toddler-activities) — it works across all three windows of the day.

## 3. Build a simple, repeated script

Toddlers respond well to a short, consistent phrase they hear every single time, rather than a different explanation each time. Something like: **"Baby is sleeping. We use whisper voices and gentle hands near the baby."** Said the same way every time — calmly, not as a scolding — it becomes a predictable cue rather than a negotiation.

Pair the script with a clear, positive redirect: "Let's go do puzzle time while baby sleeps" works better than just "don't go in there," because it gives the toddler something to do instead of just something to stop.

## 4. Offer supervised "baby time" outside of sleep windows

Much of the wandering-toward-the-nursery behavior fades when the toddler has regular, positive, supervised time with the baby while the baby is *awake* — holding a hand, singing, handing you a diaper. When toddlers get their "baby connection" needs met during awake windows, the urge to seek the baby out during sleep windows tends to decrease.

## 5. Use a visual cue the toddler can understand

Toddlers respond well to visual, concrete signals more than verbal reminders alone. A simple sign on the door (even just a picture of a moon or a sleeping face), or a colored light that's only on during naps, gives the toddler an independent way to check "is baby sleeping?" without needing to open the door to find out.

## 6. Manage the moments right before baby's nap

A lot of nursery incursions happen in the first 5–10 minutes after the baby goes down, while the toddler is still nearby from the transition. Where possible, start a toddler activity *before* you put the baby down, so the toddler is already settled into something else by the time the baby's door closes — rather than standing right outside it, unoccupied, at the exact moment the baby falls asleep.

## What to do when it happens anyway

Even with all of this in place, it will happen sometimes — that's normal, not a sign the system failed.

- Respond calmly and quietly rather than with a big reaction; toddlers often repeat behaviors that get a strong, fast response from a parent, even a frustrated one.
- Redirect immediately and physically guide the toddler out, repeating the same short script.
- If the baby does wake, resettle the baby first if possible, then address the toddler separately — trying to discipline the toddler while also comforting a crying baby usually escalates both.

## FAQ

**Is it normal for a toddler to keep doing this even after weeks of the same routine?**
Yes. Many toddlers test this repeatedly, especially during phases of increased attention-seeking (illness, a new daycare transition, a developmental leap). Consistency in the response matters more than how many times it takes.

**Should I lock the nursery door?**
A gate at the doorway is usually a better middle ground than a locked door — it prevents entry without removing the toddler's ability to see in, which can reduce curiosity-driven attempts to get inside.

**Does a shared bedroom make this worse?**
It can increase the *risk* of accidental waking (normal play noise, opening drawers), but many families successfully share a room by staggering naps and using white noise rather than needing separate rooms.
