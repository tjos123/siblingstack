Your toddler was happily the center of your world, and now there's a newborn. Suddenly they want a bottle again, won't let you out of sight, or have started pushing the baby. With less than two years between your kids, that's not bad behavior. It's a very small person with very few words trying to tell you something big.

This guide covers what jealousy looks like when the age gap is small, how to keep everyone safe, what to say in the hard moments, and when to ask for help.

**In short:** with a close age gap, jealousy usually shows up as regression and clinginess more than open aggression, though rough handling of the baby is common too. The most reliable fixes are short, predictable one-on-one time, calm simple words for feelings, and good supervision. Most of it settles within a few months.

## Why jealousy looks different with a small age gap

When siblings are under two years apart, the older child is still a toddler themselves. They're still very attached to you, and they don't yet have the language or emotional regulation to say "I miss having you to myself." So the feelings come out through behavior.

Research and parenting sources don't fully agree on whether small gaps mean more or less jealousy. Some note that a younger toddler won't remember life before the baby, while others find rivalry is more common when children are close in age and have similar needs. What they agree on is that jealousy is a normal reaction to a big change, and that reassurance and attention for the older child help. Expect some version of it whatever your gap.

## Signs of sibling jealousy in toddlers

Every child is different, but these are the patterns parents most often describe with a close age gap:

- **Regression.** Wanting a bottle or pacifier again, asking to be carried, baby talk, sudden potty-training or sleep setbacks, or wanting to be fed the way the baby is.
- **Clinginess.** A toddler who suddenly won't let you leave the room isn't being naughty. They're often checking whether your attention is still reliably theirs.
- **Attention-seeking.** Acting up specifically when you're feeding or settling the baby.
- **Rough handling or hitting.** Pushing, squeezing, grabbing, or hitting the baby. It's common at this age, because physical action is often the only way a toddler can express a big feeling.
- **Big reactions to small things.** More tantrums, more "mine!", more tears at transitions.
- **Surprising sweetness, then a swing back.** A toddler can seem fine for a week and then regress again, often alongside a new developmental stage of their own.

One point to hold onto: the regression and clinginess behaviors are usually a request for reassurance, not a sign you're doing something wrong.

## What to do when your toddler hits or is rough with the baby

Safety comes first, and it comes before any lesson.

1. **Never leave a toddler alone with a newborn,** even briefly. Sometimes even well-meaning toddlers hurt babies by accident, with hugs that squeeze too hard or a toy dropped on a face.
2. **Use physical set-ups.** Baby gates, a playpen, or a safe space for the baby make supervision much easier.
3. **Step in calmly and briefly.** Move the baby or your toddler's hand, and say something short: "I won't let you hit. Hitting hurts."
4. **Then name the feeling.** "You're upset I'm busy with the baby." Skip the long lecture, because toddlers can't take it in.
5. **Show them what to do instead.** "Gentle touches," practiced on a doll or on your arm.
6. **Give attention for the good moments.** Notice and praise gentle behavior, because toddlers repeat what gets your attention.

## What to say: simple scripts for hard moments

A short, accurate label usually works better than a long reassurance. A few examples:

- **When you're feeding the baby and your toddler is melting down:** "It's hard to wait. You wish we could play right now. When I'm done, it's your turn."
- **When the baby is the reason for a delay:** Say "my hands are busy" instead of "the baby is making us late." Avoid framing the baby as the problem.
- **When your toddler asks for a bottle again:** "You want to feel like a baby. Let's cuddle." Giving a little of what they need often passes faster than a fight about it.
- **When they refuse to be affectionate:** Don't insist on "give the baby a kiss." Forced affection builds resentment, not bonding. Offer a choice like a wave or a gentle pat.
- **When they ask whether you still love them:** "I love you just as much as before. There's lots of love for both of you."

Try to be a role model too. Toddlers copy how you speak to and touch the baby.

## How to prepare your toddler before the baby arrives

It's hard to prepare a child under about 18 months, because their understanding is limited. You can still ease the transition:

- **Make big changes early.** If your toddler is moving from a cot to a bed, do it well before the baby arrives so they don't feel the baby "took" their cot.
- **Keep their routines going,** including playgroup or activities outside the house.
- **Involve them simply.** Talk about the baby, look at photos of them as a newborn, and give them a doll to practice caring for.
- **Give them a role.** Let them pick a blanket or pass a diaper, and praise them specifically ("You helped so gently").

## One-on-one time that actually works

Predictable beats long. A short, reliable block, even five to fifteen minutes a day, usually does more for anxiety-driven behavior than an occasional long stretch.

- Pick a regular slot, such as during the baby's morning nap.
- Let your toddler choose the activity.
- Put your phone away so they get your full attention.
- Name it: "This is our special time."

## How long does sibling jealousy last?

For most families, the roughest adjustment settles within a few months, though it can ebb and flow. Regression often returns around the toddler's own developmental leaps, such as new skills or a change in routine. ## When to talk to your paediatrician

Most sibling adjustment difficulty at this age is developmentally normal. It's worth talking to your paediatrician if:

- Aggression toward the baby is frequent, intense, or not easing over time.
- Your toddler's regression is severe or lasts well beyond the first few months.
- You're finding it hard to manage your own stress. Your wellbeing matters here too.

They can help tell ordinary adjustment from something that needs more support.

## Quick checklist

- Never leave your toddler alone with the newborn, and use gates or a playpen.
- Give your toddler a predictable one-on-one block every day.
- Name feelings in short, simple words.
- Say "my hands are busy," not "the baby is the reason."
- Give mini-jobs and specific praise.
- Don't force affection.
- Expect it to ebb and flow.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal for a toddler to be jealous of a newborn?

Yes. Jealousy after a new baby arrives is very common and is generally seen as a normal reaction to a big change. It can show up as clinginess, regression, tantrums, or rough handling of the baby.

### Why is my toddler regressing since the baby came?

Regression, such as wanting a bottle, being carried, or having potty-training setbacks, is often a toddler's way of asking for reassurance that your attention is still theirs. Staying calm, offering extra cuddles, and keeping predictable one-on-one time usually helps it pass.

### Is jealousy worse with a small or large age gap?

Sources disagree, and every child is different. With a small gap, the older child has less language and is still very attached to you, so feelings tend to come out as behavior. With a bigger gap, there can be more pride or independence, but jealousy still happens.

### How long does sibling jealousy last?

For most families, the hardest adjustment eases within a few months, though regression can come and go around developmental changes. If aggression is frequent or intense, or isn't easing, check in with your paediatrician.

### What should I do if my toddler hits the baby?

Keep the baby safe first and never leave them alone together. Calmly stop the behavior with a short phrase like "I won't let you hit," name the feeling, and show a gentle alternative. Praise the gentle moments.
