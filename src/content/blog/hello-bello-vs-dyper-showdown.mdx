## The Materials Science Breakdown

## Hello Bello's Construction

Hello Bello's diapers are built around a totally chlorine-free (TCF) fluff pulp sourced from sustainably managed forests, combined with a high-performance super absorbent polymer (SAP) core, polypropylene/polyethylene/polyester nonwoven fibers for the structural layers, a plant-derived PLA (polylactic acid) nonwoven fiber, spandex strands for the leg and waist elastics, and an elastomeric film. The brand states the diapers are made without chlorine processing, artificial fragrance, phthalates, parabens, lotions, latex, or the organotin compounds TBT/DBT/MBT.

Functionally, this is a conventional-architecture diaper — fluff pulp plus SAP is the same absorbency backbone used by most mainstream disposable brands — with the plant-derived PLA nonwoven and TCF pulp sourcing as the sustainability differentiators, rather than a structurally novel diaper. The printed designs are a core part of the product identity (25+ rotating prints), which means the outer cover is a standard printed nonwoven rather than an unprinted natural-fiber layer. Hello Bello's fragrance-free product line specifically carries National Eczema Association approval, which is a relevant signal for households managing a child with documented sensitive skin, though it applies to the fragrance-free variant specifically rather than the full printed lineup.

## Dyper's Construction

Dyper's defining material claim is a diaper built with over 55% plant-based materials, with 100% natural fibers specifically at the layer touching the baby's skin — the topsheet is viscose fiber derived from bamboo, a fast-growing, low-input crop that the brand and third-party bamboo-industry sources describe as requiring no irrigation, pesticides, or fertilizer to cultivate, harvestable in 3–5 years compared to 30+ years for hardwood pulp sources. The diapers are unprinted, unscented, soft-touch, and are certified to the Standard 100 by OEKO-TEX benchmark — a widely recognized Swiss textile-safety certification that tests for a defined list of harmful substances, which is a genuine third-party verification credential rather than a brand's own claim about itself.

Dyper's marketing describes the product as free of pigments, dyes, rubber, latex, chlorine, alcohol, lotions, TBT, and phthalates. What isn't fully detailed in the publicly available product description is the specific composition of the absorbent core beneath the bamboo-viscose topsheet — the "55% plant-based" figure implies the core still relies on some proportion of standard absorbent materials (very likely a fluff-pulp-and-SAP structure similar in function to Hello Bello's, since bamboo viscose alone isn't typically used as a diaper's primary liquid-locking mechanism in the way SAP is). We're flagging this rather than guessing at the exact ratio, because Dyper's own materials disclosure focuses on the skin-contact layer and the compostability story, not a full itemized bill of materials for the core — a genuinely more transparent brand from a pure documentation standpoint would publish that breakdown the way a small number of premium competitors do, and Dyper, based on what's publicly available, doesn't appear to.

**Update, added after The independent PFAS lab testing review:** independent EPA-certified lab testing conducted after this article's original research found Dyper's diaper tested completely clean for indications of PFAS — a genuine, positive confirmation of the OEKO-TEX certification discussed above, in contrast to a same-certification competitor (Kudos) that did not test clean in the same study. Full details, including the specific testing methodology and why the result diverged from a same-certified competitor, are in [the independent PFAS lab testing review](/blog/diaper-pfas-lab-testing-review).

## Absorbency: What We Can and Can't Verify

This is the section where a lot of diaper-subscription content quietly starts inventing numbers, so it's worth being direct: **neither brand publishes independent, third-party lab-tested liquid-capacity or absorption-speed figures that we could locate**, and we're not going to assign either product a specific milliliter capacity or absorption-rate claim we can't source. What we can report:

- Independent reviewer accounts of Dyper describe the bamboo-viscose material as feeling wet somewhat faster to the touch than conventional plastic-topsheet diapers, and note it can fray if left in a very saturated state without a reliable overnight seal — a real, reported trade-off of the natural-fiber topsheet, not a defect claim we're inventing.
- Hello Bello's SAP-and-fluff-pulp architecture is functionally similar to the mainstream disposable diaper category generally, which has decades of large-scale consumer usage behind the underlying absorbency chemistry, even though Hello Bello's own specific product hasn't been independently lab-benchmarked in any source we found.
- Neither brand's own marketing makes a head-to-head liquid-capacity claim against the other, which is itself informative — if either had a clear, testable absorbency advantage, it would likely be a headline marketing point.

**For newborn liquid blowouts specifically:** the more relevant design features are poo-pocket construction, leg-cuff design, and waistband stretch, not topsheet material — and neither company's public documentation gives a detailed comparative breakdown of these structural features against each other. Parent-reported experience (rather than lab data) suggests fit consistency and correct sizing matter more for blowout prevention than the topsheet fiber source, which tracks with general disposable-diaper engineering knowledge — a diaper that fits snugly at the leg and waist will outperform a looser diaper made of "better" material every time.

**For heavy overnight toddler wetting:** this is the scenario most likely to expose a real difference between the two products, precisely because of the reported faster-wet-feeling and fray risk noted for Dyper's bamboo viscose topsheet under heavy saturation. A toddler who is a genuinely heavy overnight wetter is the profile most likely to test the limits of Dyper's standard (non-overnight-specific) product line, though we did not find a dedicated "overnight" SKU variant clearly documented for Dyper the way some competitors offer. Households in this situation should treat this as a real open question to test directly with a trial pack rather than something either brand's marketing will resolve for you.

## The Compostability Difference — a Genuine, Verifiable Advantage for Dyper

Where Dyper has an unambiguous, well-documented structural advantage is end-of-life disposal. The entire diaper is described as compostable, and the company operates REDYPER, a program allowing subscribers to bag and ship used diapers back to Dyper (or its composting partner, TerraCycle, per some program descriptions) for centralized commercial composting rather than landfill disposal. Reporting on this program's cost has varied across sources and years — some older coverage described a separate monthly REDYPER fee (reported around $50/month in older material), while a more recently updated review describes the service as included at no additional charge. Given that conflict, we'd rather flag it plainly than assert either figure as current: **confirm REDYPER's current cost structure directly with Dyper before assuming it's free or budgeting for a $50/month add-on**, since our sources genuinely disagree and we don't want to guess on a detail that materially affects your monthly cost.

Hello Bello does not market a composting or take-back program for used diapers; standard disposal is landfill, as with most mainstream disposable diaper brands. For a household where end-of-life environmental impact is a primary decision driver — not just the raw material sourcing, but what happens to the product after use — this is the single clearest differentiator in Dyper's favor, and it's backed by an actual operating program rather than aspirational marketing language.

## The Supply Chain and Shipping Audit

## Transit Speed and Processing

Hello Bello processes orders within 1–2 business days (orders placed after 1 p.m. PDT may roll to the next business day), with delivery typically arriving 3–7 business days after processing for the continental US — longer for Hawaii, Alaska (up to 21 days), and APO/FPO addresses (1–8 weeks depending on base location).

Dyper's published delivery structure describes orders shipping within two to three days of placement, with typical first-box arrival in under a week domestically. Beyond the standard cycle, Dyper offers an SOS reordering option intended for households that are about to run out before their next scheduled shipment — the specific turnaround window for SOS orders has been described in older company materials as a matter of hours rather than days, though we'd treat any specific hour count from older press material as potentially stale and recommend confirming current SOS turnaround directly rather than planning around a multi-year-old figure.

## Box Customizability and Delivery Frequency

This is a genuine point of difference. Dyper's delivery frequency is adjustable across a wide range — from every 2 weeks up to every 12 weeks — giving households significant control over how much inventory arrives per shipment without changing the box's contents. Hello Bello's frequency adjustment is also available through account settings, but the brand's own materials don't advertise a specific range as wide as Dyper's 2-to-12-week spread; Hello Bello's system is generally described around the standard monthly cycle with the ability to adjust the date, rather than marketed as a wide-range frequency dial the way Dyper's is.

For a multi-diapering household trying to solve the volume-matching problem covered in the portal-optimization piece of this series, Dyper's wider frequency range is a genuine practical advantage if you're trying to compress delivery cycles to keep pace with a high-consumption newborn without over-ordering on a slower-consuming older sibling's size.

## Shipping Cost

Hello Bello charges $5.95 for orders under $35 and $1.95 for orders of $35 or more, based on published account documentation, with a $25 surcharge for Hawaii and Alaska deliveries. Dyper advertises free shipping on its standard box, with applicable sales tax added based on delivery location — meaning Dyper's headline box price is closer to the true out-of-pocket cost than Hello Bello's, which typically adds a small shipping charge on top of the bundle price.

## Return and Exchange Policy for Outgrown Sizes

This is an area where neither brand publishes a clearly detailed, specific policy for unopened-pack returns or exchanges when a child rapidly outgrows a size — a real and common scenario in fast-growing infants, and one this guide would be doing readers a disservice to guess at. General subscription-commerce norms (and Hello Bello's own emphasis on being able to freely adjust size, frequency, and contents from the account dashboard before the next order ships) suggest the more reliable strategy for both brands is **proactive resizing before your next shipment processes**, rather than relying on a post-purchase return for a pack that's already shipped in the wrong size. If a formal return/exchange policy for unopened packs exists at either company, it wasn't clearly documented in the sources available for this guide — check each brand's current customer service policy directly before assuming either will accept an unopened pack back.

## Trial Options and the Real Cost of Switching Brands Mid-Subscription

For a multi-diapering household, committing to a brand and then discovering a fit or absorbency problem three weeks in is a materially bigger disruption than it would be for a single-child household — you're not just re-shopping one size, you're potentially re-shopping your entire monthly diaper logistics for multiple children at once. This makes trial-friendliness a genuinely practical factor, not just a nice-to-have.

Historically, both brands have offered some form of discounted or free first-order trial mechanism — Hello Bello has run free-gift and percentage-off first-bundle promotions (25% off in some past campaigns, per subscriber-review coverage), and Dyper has been referenced in review coverage as offering a free trial period in at least some enrollment paths. Neither brand's _current, standing_ trial offer terms were independently confirmed in the sources used for this guide, and promotional terms in this category change frequently enough (new-customer percentage discounts, free gift thresholds, referral credits) that any specific figure cited here risks being stale by the time you read it. The actionable takeaway: **check the live signup page for both brands' current new-subscriber offer before committing**, and treat any trial discount as a bonus on top of the cost comparison in this guide, not a reason to skip doing that comparison.

**On switching mid-subscription:** both brands' account-management structure (covered in more detail in [the Diaper Bundle Dashboard guide](/blog/diaper-bundle-portal-mixing-sizes-blueprint)) allows adjusting size, frequency, and contents without cancelling outright, which lowers the switching cost within a brand. Switching _between_ Hello Bello and Dyper entirely — abandoning one subscription and starting the other — carries no penalty beyond whatever's left unused in your most recent box, based on available cancellation-policy information, though Honest Company-style phone-required cancellation friction (discussed in the Diaper Bundle Dashboard guide) doesn't appear to be a factor for either brand covered here; both are described as manageable through self-service account settings.

## Packaging and Outer Shipping Materials

A dimension of the sustainability comparison that's easy to overlook because it's not the diaper itself: what the box and packaging are made of. Hello Bello states its shipping boxes are fully recyclable, and actively markets the box-upcycling angle (turning the shipping box itself into a play structure) as part of the unboxing experience — a form of reuse that extends the packaging's life before it enters the waste stream at all. Neither company's standard outer shipping box (as opposed to the REDYPER-specific return packaging) was described in available sources as anything other than conventional recyclable cardboard, so this isn't a meaningful point of differentiation on the outbound side — the difference shows up specifically in what happens to the box on the _return_ leg of Dyper's composting program, which Hello Bello has no equivalent to since it doesn't operate a take-back program at all.
