## The Kudos Origin Story: What Built the Reputation This Controversy Is Now Testing

Kudos was founded by Amrita Saigal, a former Procter & Gamble engineer and MIT graduate, in 2018 or 2019 depending on the source consulted, and is headquartered in Boston, Massachusetts. The company's founding proposition — a disposable diaper with a 100% cotton topsheet rather than the plastic or plant-derived nonwoven topsheet used by virtually every competitor, including the other three brands in this cluster — was, according to Saigal's own public statements, explicitly benchmarked against the industry leader rather than positioned as a niche alternative. "I'm not launching a product that is not at par or better than Pampers," Saigal told CNBC in August 2024.

That claim had real independent backing at the time. NBC News reported in August 2024 that Kudos commissioned independent testing through Diaper Testing International, which found the product absorbed more fluid than Pampers Pure Protection, Huggies Special Delivery, and Honest Company's diaper. NBC's reporting also captured a response from an Honest Company spokesperson, who noted the brand's own testing philosophy emphasizes in-use performance (comfort, fit, and leak protection together) over absorbency figures in isolation — a reasonable methodological pushback, and useful context that this was a genuine, reported comparison rather than an uncontested marketing claim.

**This is an important detail for evaluating the 2026 complaints, and it cuts in a specific direction: the independent testing that established Kudos's absorbency reputation was conducted on the pre-2026 product.** It has no bearing on whether the redesigned diaper reviewers describe starting in March 2026 performs the same way, and nothing in the sources available for this article indicates that testing has been repeated on the current version.

By the time of that August 2024 reporting, Kudos had raised more than $6 million in funding, including a $3 million round from Precursor Ventures, Xfund, and Oversubscribed Ventures, had sold more than 20 million diapers in the preceding twelve months, and had grown sales more than 100% year-over-year. The brand had also just landed its first major retail placement: 375 Target stores as of August 2024, a milestone Saigal described to CNBC as personally significant given how selectively Target curates its baby-care shelf space.

Modern Retail's reporting doesn't specify a location for the new manufacturing line, and no subsequent company statement confirming or denying a Mexico location was located for this article. ## Materials Science: What Likely Changed, As Best the Evidence Allows

The Kudos cotton-premium breakdown documented Kudos's original construction: a U.S.-sourced, dry-processed 100% cotton topsheet (a proprietary formulation the company markets as TruCotton, per a detailed third-party review from gimmethegoodstuff.org), a total chlorine-free wood pulp core, "DoubleDry" dual-layer absorbency technology, OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certification, and FSC-certified materials with water-based, heavy-metal-free inks. The same gimmethegoodstuff.org review made an argument worth weighing against [the Hello Bello vs. Dyper audit](/blog/hello-bello-vs-dyper-showdown)'s treatment of Dyper's bamboo viscose: that converting bamboo into viscose fiber is itself an energy- and chemical-intensive process, and that well-sourced cotton may be the cleaner natural-fiber choice by comparison — a genuine materials-science counterpoint this cluster hadn't previously included.

The 2026 complaints center on three specific, repeated descriptors: **thinner**, **narrower**, and **scratchy**, plus a specific failure mode — core material (fluff pulp and superabsorbent polymer) becoming visible and escaping the product during heavy overnight use, which one reviewer's child experienced at 19 lbs in a Size 3 rated to 24 lbs, well within the stated weight range rather than at its upper edge. That specific detail — a failure within the stated size range, not at its boundary — is meaningful, because it rules out simple user sizing error as the explanation and points toward an actual reduction in core material volume or a change in core construction.

**What we cannot determine from available sources:** whether the cotton topsheet itself changed, whether the change is concentrated in the absorbent core beneath it, or whether it's a combination of both. No source available for this research includes a manufacturer statement specifying what changed, and no independent lab has (as far as we could determine) re-run the Diaper Testing International-style comparison on the current product. A parent trying to evaluate this today is, at this point, relying on consistent anecdotal pattern-matching across independent reviewers rather than a confirmed technical specification change — which is genuinely useful information, but categorically different from a lab-verified fact, and this article isn't going to blur that distinction.

## The Business Logic Behind a Retail-Driven Manufacturing Change

It's worth understanding _why_ a company in Kudos's specific position would be under real pressure to change its manufacturing approach, separate from whether it actually did. Saigal's own words to Modern Retail are direct about the mechanics: a direct-to-consumer subscription brand and a national mass retailer operate under fundamentally different supply-chain expectations. A subscription-only brand can absorb some variability in production timing because it controls its own delivery cadence and can communicate directly with subscribers about delays. A brand stocked in hundreds of Target stores cannot — a retailer at that scale requires consistent, on-time, high-volume fulfillment, and a shortfall risks the shelf placement itself, not just a delayed shipment to one household. "You can kind of live and die by getting the product where it needs to be on time," as Saigal put it.

This is a genuine, well-documented tension in the consumer packaged goods industry broadly, not a claim specific to Kudos: scaling a premium, small-batch product to mass-retail volume commonly requires either a second production facility, a different supplier relationship, or process changes to hit volume targets — and any of those paths can, even without any intent to cut corners, introduce subtle specification drift if quality-control processes aren't scaled with equal rigor. This context doesn't prove that's what happened here. It does explain why the timeline — a documented capacity expansion in early-to-mid 2025, followed by documented product complaints beginning roughly a year later — is a pattern worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as coincidence, even in the continued absence of a company confirmation of cause.

It's also worth naming the incentive structure this creates for how a company might respond, or decline to respond, to complaints like these. A public statement confirming a manufacturing change carries real risk for a brand whose entire value proposition is trust and material integrity — acknowledging a cost-driven or capacity-driven specification change cuts directly against that positioning in a way it might not for a brand built on price or convenience instead. This doesn't mean Kudos's silence (as of this article's research) reflects anything in particular; companies decline to comment on unconfirmed customer complaints for many reasons, including simply not having identified a root cause yet. But readers should understand that the absence of a statement is not, by itself, informative in either direction.

## How the Industry Has Handled Redesign Backlash Before

Kudos isn't the first diaper brand to face a wave of complaints following a design change, and looking at a clearly separate, historical precedent is useful for calibrating expectations about how this kind of situation typically unfolds — with the explicit caveat that the following case involves an entirely different company, product, and decade, and is included only as industry context, not as evidence of anything about Kudos.

In 2010 and 2011, Procter & Gamble faced a significant consumer backlash after redesigning Pampers Cruisers and Swaddlers, with some parents reporting their babies developed blisters and rashes attributed to the new design. The Consumer Product Safety Commission investigated the complaints and, according to the agency's own spokesperson at the time, found "no need for corrective action" and did not issue a recall. P&G's own external relations director characterized the redesign as part of the company's regular product-improvement cycle rather than a response to the complaints, stating the company updates its products every year or so as a matter of course.

Two things from that precedent are worth carrying into how a reader evaluates the current Kudos situation. First, a CPSC investigation without a resulting recall is a real, meaningful outcome — it indicates a regulator looked at complaint volume and severity and didn't find grounds for mandatory action, which is different from a company being cleared of the underlying complaints entirely; consumer dissatisfaction and regulatory recall thresholds are different bars. Second, a company declining to attribute a redesign to consumer complaints — characterizing it instead as routine product iteration — is a documented pattern in this industry, meaning the absence of an acknowledgment from Kudos (as of this article's research) is, again, not unusual or uniquely evasive behavior for this category. **None of this means the Kudos situation will resolve the same way, or that a CPSC investigation is warranted here** — no evidence of CPSC involvement with Kudos was found in this research, and this section exists purely to give readers a documented reference point for how this kind of consumer-complaint pattern has played out elsewhere in the same product category, not to suggest an equivalence between the two situations.
