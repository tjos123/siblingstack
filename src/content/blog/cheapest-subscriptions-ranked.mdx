**Bottom line up front:** The lowest _advertised_ per-unit price and the lowest _actual_ monthly bill are two different rankings, because minimum order sizes and shipping fees change the real entry cost significantly. Little Spoon's Babyblends advertise the lowest starting per-unit price in this cluster (as low as $2.80/unit at the highest volume tier), but Once Upon a Farm's and Cerebelly's pouches often produce a lower _realistic minimum monthly spend_ for a household that doesn't want to commit to Little Spoon's higher-volume tiers. There is no single "cheapest" answer independent of how much your household actually needs to order — this guide ranks both dimensions honestly rather than picking one number to lead with.

## 2. Why the Advertised Number Isn't the Real Number

Every "lowest price" in the table above requires ordering at that specific company's highest volume tier. For a household that doesn't actually need that much food, the _effective_ price per unit is higher, and the _total_ monthly commitment is higher too — meaning the lowest-advertised-price product isn't automatically the cheapest realistic option for every household.

**Little Spoon's structure rewards volume most aggressively.** The jump from its smallest Babyblends tier ($3.80–$4.05/unit at 14 units) to its largest ($2.80–$3.05/unit at 42 units) is a real, roughly 25–30% discount — but it requires committing to 3 blends a day, which not every infant needs, especially one still transitioning from primarily milk-based feeding.

**Tiny Organics has a real minimum order floor.** Its 12-cup minimum order, even at the lower per-unit rate available at that tier ($4.69–$5.49), still requires a **minimum spend of roughly $56–$66 plus $5.99 shipping** every delivery cycle — there's no way to order a smaller trial quantity to test the product at a lower total commitment.

**Nurture Life's $39 order minimum plus a real shipping fee below that threshold** ($6–$8.99, waived at $59+) means a household ordering a small quantity to test the service pays a real premium on both the per-unit price and the shipping fee simultaneously, until the order size crosses that threshold.

## 4. What This Ranking Doesn't Capture

**Promotional pricing changes all of this short-term.** As covered in the companion switching-subscriptions guide, most of these companies run recurring first-order discount codes (sometimes 30–50% off), which can temporarily invert this entire ranking for a new customer's first month. This guide's ranking reflects standard, ongoing pricing — not promotional rates, which are worth checking at the time you're ready to order, separately from this comparison.

**Shipping cost per delivery matters more for infrequent, small orders.** A household ordering the smallest tier from any company pays the same flat shipping fee as a household ordering a much larger tier, which means shipping represents a proportionally larger share of the total cost at small order sizes — another reason the "smallest tier" price in Section 3 isn't simply the largest-tier price divided down proportionally.

**This ranking doesn't factor in food waste.** As covered in the companion food-strikes guide, the cheapest advertised price becomes irrelevant if a meaningful share of what's ordered goes uneaten — a slightly more expensive product a child reliably eats can be cheaper in practice than a nominally cheaper one that's frequently rejected.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Little Spoon actually the cheapest option in this cluster?**
It has the lowest advertised per-unit price for Babyblends at its highest volume tier, but that requires committing to 3 blends a day; for a lighter, trial-level commitment, Once Upon a Farm's or Cerebelly's pouches typically produce a lower total monthly spend.

**Does ordering the largest tier always save money overall, even if my child doesn't eat everything?**
No — the per-unit savings at larger tiers only translate into real savings if the additional volume is actually consumed; ordering more than your child eats to chase a lower per-unit rate can cost more in total than a smaller order at a higher per-unit price, once wasted, unused product is factored in.

**Are there hidden fees beyond the advertised per-unit price I should watch for?**
Shipping is the main one across every company in this cluster, ranging roughly $5.99–$12 depending on order size and company, and some companies waive it above a spending threshold; always check the total at checkout, including shipping, rather than comparing per-unit prices alone.

**Do promotional discount codes change this ranking significantly?**
Yes, often substantially for a first order — recurring promotional codes in this category (see the companion switching-subscriptions guide) can temporarily make a normally pricier company cheaper than a normally cheaper one; this guide's ranking reflects standard ongoing pricing, not promotional rates.

**Is Cerebelly's retail availability actually cheaper than its subscription?**
This varies — retail pricing at stores like Walmart or Target can sometimes be lower than direct subscription pricing depending on current in-store promotions, but subscription pricing includes the convenience of scheduled delivery; compare actual current prices at checkout for your specific situation rather than assuming one channel is reliably cheaper.

**Should I choose the cheapest option even if it doesn't match my child's nutritional needs as well?**
Cost is one factor among several covered across this cluster — the companion brand-philosophy guides address nutritional and developmental fit, which matters alongside cost rather than instead of it; this ranking is meant to inform a budget decision, not override the fit considerations covered elsewhere.

**How often do these companies change their pricing?**
This guide's research reflects pricing current as of its own research point; subscription pricing in this category changes periodically, so always verify current per-unit and shipping costs directly with each company before finalizing a budget based on this ranking.

**Is it cheaper to combine a low-cost pouch brand with a higher-cost toddler-meal brand than to use one company for both children?**
Based on the tier structures in this cluster, yes, often — since infant purée products and toddler textured meals are priced and tiered independently of each other regardless of company, mixing a lower-cost pouch brand for the younger sibling with whichever toddler-meal brand best fits the older sibling's needs (per the companion brand-comparison guides) is a reasonable way to manage combined cost without sacrificing fit for either child.
