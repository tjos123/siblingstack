A small age gap creates a strange milestone experience: for a while, your two kids can look almost the same size, and then within a matter of months the gap becomes obvious again as the younger one's development catches up developmentally but not chronologically. Here's a realistic picture of what that timeline tends to look like, with the standard caveat that every kid's timeline varies and a pediatrician is the right source for anything specific to your child.

## Why the gap feels like it's constantly changing size

With roughly a 10-month gap, your older child is hitting toddler milestones — walking, early words, more complex play — right around the time your newborn is just beginning theirs. For the first several months after the second baby arrives, the practical gap between them (in terms of what they can each physically do) is enormous, even though the calendar gap stays fixed. Then, as the younger one moves through infancy into toddlerhood, the *functional* gap shrinks for a while — an 18-month-old and a 28-month-old are doing a lot of overlapping activities — before widening again as the older one moves into preschool-level skills.

## The early period (0–12 months for the younger child)

This is the stretch where the gap feels biggest. Your older child is mobile, communicative, and increasingly independent, while your younger one is working through the standard early milestones — head control, rolling, sitting, early babbling — on their own typical timeline. It's common for parents to feel like they're parenting two completely different households during this period, because in terms of daily needs, they largely are.

## The convergence period (roughly 12–24 months for the younger child)

This is often where the age gap starts to feel smaller in practice, even though it never changes on paper. Your younger child is walking, talking in simple words or phrases, and increasingly able to participate in the same activities as your older sibling, who's likely somewhere in the 22–34 month range during this window — both genuinely in "toddlerhood," even if at different points within it. This is frequently the stretch where parents report the age gap feeling like an advantage: built-in playmates, overlapping interests, shared activities that don't require completely different setups.

## What tends to cause comparison stress, and why it's usually not meaningful

It's extremely common to catch yourself comparing the younger child's pace against the older one's at the same age — "my first was walking by now" or "my older one talked earlier." With a small gap, this comparison is unusually easy to make because the memory of the older child's milestones is so recent. It's worth naming this pattern explicitly so you can catch yourself doing it: individual developmental timelines vary normally by a meaningful margin, and a younger sibling hitting a milestone a few weeks or months later than an older sibling did is almost always within completely normal range, not a signal of anything.

## When timing differences are worth mentioning to your pediatrician

Routine well-child visits already screen for developmental milestones at standard intervals, and that screening — not a sibling comparison — is the right basis for whether something needs a closer look. If you notice a significant, sustained gap between your younger child's development and standard milestone guidelines (not just compared to your older child, but compared to typical ranges for their actual age), that's worth raising at your next visit, or sooner if you're concerned. Comparing siblings against each other is a normal, low-stakes thing parents do; using standard milestone charts for your younger child's actual age, and your pediatrician's assessment, is the more reliable signal if something feels genuinely off.

## The honest takeaway

The age gap will feel enormous, then comfortably small, then differently sized again as both kids keep growing — and almost none of that shifting size has anything to do with whether either kid is developing typically. It's mostly just what a small, fixed calendar gap looks like applied to two kids who are each growing at their own normal pace.
