"Be consistent" is the most repeated piece of discipline advice there is, and it's mostly written for parents with kids close enough in age and stage that the same rule applies to both the same way. That's not the situation when one kid is an opinionated three-year-old and the other is barely crawling. Consistency doesn't mean identical treatment here — it means something narrower, and figuring out what it actually means is the real work.

:::section
section-adult|Consistency is about the adult, not the rule
badge:🎯 The Real Standard
badge:⚖️ Adjusted, Not Identical
The version of consistency that actually holds up with a wide developmental gap isn't "both kids get the same consequence for the same behavior." It's "the same behavior gets the same *response from you*, adjusted for what each kid can understand and do." A toddler who hits gets a clear verbal boundary and a redirection. An infant who grabs and pulls hair isn't being defiant — there's no discipline response appropriate there at all, just physical redirection. Both responses come from the same underlying standard (hurting people isn't okay), even though they look nothing alike.
:::

:::section
section-safety|When the toddler gets physical with the baby, safety comes before the lesson
badge:🛡️ Safety First
badge:🔄 Practiced Sequence
This is worth having a clear, practiced sequence for, because it happens fast and the instinct to launch straight into correcting the toddler can mean the baby stays in reach a beat longer than they should. The order that holds up under pressure: separate the two kids physically first, then attend to whichever one got hurt, and only after that address the toddler's behavior. A toddler hitting or grabbing at a sibling — even a toddler who's generally gentle — is a near-universal phase, not a sign of a bad temperament or a parenting failure; the goal in the moment isn't to make them feel like a bad kid, it's to interrupt the behavior calmly and keep the baby out of reach while you do. Comforting the baby visibly, in front of the toddler, also does some of the teaching on its own — it shows where your concern goes when someone gets hurt, without you needing to say much at all.
:::

:::warning
**Safety comes before the lesson, every time.** The instinct to correct the toddler first is understandable, but it leaves the baby in reach a beat longer. Separate, comfort, then correct — in that order.

:::section
section-triggers|Know the common triggers, since prevention beats correction here
badge:⏰ Tired & Hungry Windows
badge:👀 Supervision
Toddler aggression toward a sibling is rarely random — it usually clusters around a small set of triggers: being tired, hungry, overstimulated, or simply not having the words yet to say what they want. None of this excuses hitting or biting, but recognizing the pattern changes what you do about it. If your toddler tends to get physical with the baby specifically in the hour before a nap or right before dinner, that's a scheduling and supervision issue as much as a discipline one — closer supervision or a snack offered proactively in that window will prevent more incidents than any consequence delivered after the fact.
:::

:::section
section-outlet|Give the physical impulse somewhere else to go
badge:🏃 Physical Outlet
A toddler who's hitting or grabbing usually has real frustration underneath it and genuinely limited ways to discharge it yet. Alongside the verbal boundary, offering a specific physical outlet — stomping feet, hitting a pillow, squeezing a stress ball — gives the impulse somewhere to go instead of just telling them to stop and leaving the underlying feeling with nowhere to land. This isn't a reward for hitting; it's teaching the more acceptable version of the same physical release.
:::

:::section
section-praise|Notice and name the gentle moments, not just the rough ones
badge:💚 Specific Praise
It's easy for interactions with a toddler around a new sibling to become entirely reactive — correcting hitting, redirecting grabbing — and never mentioning anything when it goes well. Toddlers respond strongly to specific, immediate praise ("you were so gentle petting the baby's hand just now") in a way that shapes future behavior more effectively than correction alone. If every interaction with the baby only generates parental attention when something goes wrong, that's an incentive structure worth noticing and counterbalancing on purpose.
:::

:::section
section-alignment|Get both caregivers on the same page, not just yourself
badge:🤝 Co-Parent Alignment
badge:🔁 Same Read
Consistency across a developmental gap is hard enough with one adult; it gets harder when a partner, grandparent, or other regular caregiver responds differently to the same behavior. A toddler learns fast whether "no hitting the baby" is an actual rule or just what happens to apply when one specific adult is in the room. This doesn't require a formal agreement, but it's worth a short, explicit conversation with anyone else regularly present — what the response to toddler aggression toward the baby actually looks like, so the toddler isn't getting a different read on the same behavior depending on who's watching.
:::

:::section
section-unfairness|The toddler will notice the "unfairness" — name it before they do
badge:⚖️ Fairness Framing
Once a toddler is old enough to notice that the baby "gets away with" things they'd get corrected for, that observation is coming whether you address it or not. Naming it proactively — "the baby doesn't know the rule yet, you're still teaching them just like I taught you" — tends to land better than waiting for a toddler to raise it as a grievance mid-conflict, when they're already upset and less able to hear an explanation calmly.
:::

:::section
section-competition|Watch for discipline drifting into competition management
badge:🧑⚖️ Referee Trap
A common trap with two kids at different stages is discipline conversations quietly turning into referee duty — who started it, who had it first, who's "really" at fault. With an infant in the mix, that framing breaks down fast, because the infant genuinely isn't a participant in the same sense. It helps to separate two different jobs: managing the toddler's behavior (which is real discipline) and managing physical safety around the infant (which is supervision, not discipline). Treating both as "discipline" tends to escalate situations that are really just a toddler needing more hands-on adult involvement, not a consequence.
:::

:::section
section-fairness|One incident, one household: how to think about "fairness" out loud
badge:🗣️ Script & Framing
When toddlers ask "why does the baby not get in trouble," a short, matter-of-fact answer tends to work better than a long justification. Something like: everyone gets treated fairly for what they understand and can do — the baby will get real rules too, once they're old enough to learn them, the same way the toddler did. Over-explaining or over-apologizing for the difference can accidentally signal that the unequal treatment *is* actually unfair, which undermines the point.
:::

:::section
section-simple|Keep your own rules simple enough to apply under pressure
badge:✅ Fewer, Clearer Responses
The more nuanced a household's discipline system gets, the harder it is to apply consistently in an actual moment of chaos — which is most moments with two kids at different stages. A small number of clear, repeatable responses (a calm verbal boundary, a physical redirection, a short break from an activity) held consistently beats an elaborate system that's too complicated to execute when you're also holding a baby.
:::

:::checklist
📋 What to Actually Let Go Of
- **A toddler complaining about "unfairness" between siblings** is developmentally normal and not a sign your approach is wrong. It usually just needs a calm, short answer, repeated as many times as it comes up.
- **You won't always get the tone right in the moment** — sometimes you'll sound stricter with the toddler than you meant to, simply because they're the one who can understand words. That's a normal side effect of the age gap, not a bias to fix.
- **Discipline consistency doesn't mean equal *outcomes* every time.** It means the same values applied at each kid's actual level of understanding — which will look different for years to come, not just right now.
- **Occasional hitting or grabbing toward a sibling** is a near-universal toddler phase, not a preview of their character. It's worth taking seriously in the moment without carrying long-term worry about it between incidents.
:::

## Common questions

**My toddler keeps hitting the baby — is that a sign of a bigger problem?**
No, not on its own. Hitting and biting are developmentally typical for children under three, especially amid a major household change like a new sibling, and they're a training issue rather than a behavioral red flag. Consistent, calm intervention over time is what resolves it, not a single conversation.

**Should I punish the toddler the same way I'd correct an older child?**
Not usually — toddlers respond better to immediate, concrete redirection and clear limits than to reasoning or delayed consequences, since they don't yet have the cognitive tools to connect a later consequence back to the original behavior.

**What if my co-parent responds differently to the same behavior?**
Worth a short, direct conversation outside the heat of the moment about what the standard response should be — a toddler will quickly learn to read which adult enforces what, which undermines consistency more than any single inconsistent moment does.
