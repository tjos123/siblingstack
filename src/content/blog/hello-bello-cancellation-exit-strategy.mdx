## The Regulatory Landscape: Why Cancellation Friction Exists At All

A household frustrated by inconsistent or unclear cancellation mechanics across subscription brands is often assuming there's a federal rule requiring cancellation to be as easy as signup. As of this writing, that assumption is inaccurate, and it's worth understanding precisely why, since it directly explains why brands in this category aren't uniformly held to a "click to cancel" standard.

In October 2024, the FTC finalized amendments to its Negative Option Rule — widely known as the "click-to-cancel" rule — which would have required cancellation to be at least as simple as enrollment, along with clearer disclosure of subscription terms and express consent before billing. In July 2025, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated that rule in its entirety in _Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC_, on procedural grounds — the court held the FTC hadn't completed the preliminary regulatory analysis required for a rule of that economic scope, and that a later analysis couldn't retroactively fix the defect. As a result, the rule that would have mandated one-click cancellation across the industry is not currently in force. The FTC reopened rulemaking on negative option plans in March 2026, and consumer advocacy groups have petitioned for the click-to-cancel provision to be revived as part of that new proceeding, but as of this writing, no replacement rule has taken effect.

**What actually governs subscription cancellation practices right now**, in the absence of a functioning click-to-cancel rule, is a narrower set of protections: the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA), Section 5 of the FTC Act's general prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices, and individual state auto-renewal statutes, which vary by state. None of these guarantees the specific "as easy to cancel as to sign up" standard the vacated rule would have required — which is precisely why the self-service-but-undocumented-cutoff pattern described throughout this article isn't a Hello Bello-specific failure so much as a reflection of the current, genuinely unsettled regulatory environment for subscription commerce broadly. This context doesn't change the practical steps in this guide, but it explains why a published, numbered cancellation deadline isn't something you're legally guaranteed to find on any given subscription brand's site right now, Hello Bello included.
