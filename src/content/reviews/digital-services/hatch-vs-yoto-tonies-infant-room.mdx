## Why "Just Get a Sound Machine" Isn't the Full Answer

The instinct to solve an infant's sleep-sound needs with the first white-noise machine on a baby registry misses a genuinely well-documented safety issue that most product marketing doesn't foreground. A 2014 study published in the AAP's own journal, _Pediatrics_, tested 14 popular infant sleep machines at 30, 100, and 200 centimeters from a simulated 6-month-old's ear canal. The finding: every single device tested exceeded 50 A-weighted decibels — the sound level standard used in hospital newborn nurseries — at the closest tested distance. Some models exceeded 85 decibels at maximum volume, a level the World Health Organization associates with hearing-damage risk under prolonged exposure.

Following that research and a 2023 policy reiteration, the AAP's practical guidance settled on a small number of concrete rules: keep sound machine output at or below roughly 50 dB as measured at the crib, position the device at least 7 feet (about 200 cm) from the baby's head, and avoid running it continuously all night at high volume — turn it down or off once the child is asleep where practical. Worth noting honestly: not every health authority lands on an identical number. The AAP's nursery-derived 50 dB figure is the most commonly cited benchmark, while the CDC's general pediatric noise-exposure guidance references a 60 dB threshold — a modest discrepancy between two credible sources rather than a single, universally agreed number, and a reason to treat 50 dB as the more conservative, better-cited target rather than treating either figure as the sole correct answer.

This is the actual axis this comparison needs to run on — not "which brand sounds nicer," but "which setup can you realistically keep inside that range in your specific room."

## Testing Your Actual Setup, Regardless of Brand

Since no manufacturer in this comparison publishes a verified, independently audited safe-volume guarantee, the practical safety step is the same no matter which device ends up in the room:

- Set the device to the volume level you actually intend to use overnight — not the demo or maximum setting.
- Place a free decibel-meter app (widely available for both major smartphone platforms) at the approximate height and location of the baby's head in the crib.
- Confirm the reading sits at or below roughly 50 dB, adjusting the volume down or increasing distance from the crib until it does.
- Re-check after any volume change, furniture rearrangement, or room switch — surfaces, carpeting, and room size all measurably affect the actual sound level reaching the crib, per Hatch's own stated guidance on this point, and there's no reason to expect this physics to behave differently for a competing brand's speaker.
- Where practical, turn the device down or off once the child is asleep rather than running it at full overnight volume for the entire night, consistent with AAP guidance on limiting duration of exposure, not just peak volume.

Community-reported settings (from parent forums discussing Hatch specifically) commonly cluster around 25–30% volume at 7–10 feet of distance as producing a comfortable, sub-50dB result for many rooms — offered here as anecdotal, real-world reference points from actual users rather than as a verified substitute for testing your own specific room, since carpeting, ceiling height, and device placement all shift the actual number.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the AAP recommend against using sound machines for infants entirely?**
No — the AAP's guidance is about safe use (volume at or below roughly 50 dB, placed at least 7 feet away, limited duration), not a recommendation against sound machines altogether; used within those parameters, white noise is broadly considered a reasonable and common sleep aid.

**If Hatch's own support page says their device stays under 50 dB at 5–10 feet, why does independent testing show 96.3 dB?**
The two figures aren't necessarily contradictory — Hatch's stated 50 dB reference applies specifically to typical real-world placement (5–10 feet, carpeted room) at whatever volume setting a parent has chosen, while the 96.3 dB figure from independent lab testing reflects the device's maximum possible output at its highest volume setting and likely a closer measurement distance; the practical takeaway is that the device is capable of far exceeding safe levels if set too high or placed too close, which is exactly why testing your own actual setup matters more than either published number alone.

**Can I use a Yoto Player's radio feature as a full white-noise replacement, or is it missing dedicated white-noise content?**
Yoto's built-in sleep-sound and radio options are generally usable as ambient/calming background audio, though the specific content library available through this free, card-free function can change over time — check the current app for what's available rather than assuming a fixed permanent lineup.

**Why do Tonies' Sleepy Friends figures say "age 3+" if they're being marketed for infants and toddlers?**
This is a genuine discrepancy between the company's broader marketing language (positioning the Toniebox as suitable "for the littlest ones") and the specific age guidance printed on individual Sleepy Friends product listings; a household considering Tonies specifically for a young infant's room should weigh the printed age recommendation directly rather than relying on the general marketing framing alone.

**Does running a sound machine all night affect a baby's ability to learn to fall asleep without it later?**
This is a developmental and behavioral question outside the scope of a cost-and-safety comparison guide; the AAP's practical suggestion to turn the device off or down once a child is asleep, rather than running it at full volume continuously, is offered as general guidance, and specific concerns about sleep-association dependency are worth discussing with a pediatrician or sleep consultant.

**Is the Hatch Rest+'s two-way audio monitor a substitute for a dedicated baby video monitor?**
No — it provides basic audio monitoring as a secondary feature bundled with the sound machine, not the video coverage, split-screen multi-room viewing, or dedicated monitoring-specific features of a purpose-built baby monitor; households prioritizing comprehensive room monitoring should treat this as a bonus feature rather than a full replacement.

**If I already have a decibel meter reading under 50 dB at my Hatch's normal volume setting, do I still need to re-test if I move the device?**
Yes — the same volume setting can produce a meaningfully different decibel reading at the crib depending on the room's size, wall surfaces, carpeting, and the device's new distance from the baby, so re-testing after any placement change is the only way to confirm the new setup is still within a safe range.

**Does Hatch+'s $4.99/month subscription apply per device, or does it cover multiple Hatch units in the same household?**
This is an account-and-billing detail that can vary by current subscription terms; confirm directly with Hatch whether a single Hatch+ subscription covers multiple registered devices in one household or requires a separate subscription per unit before assuming either structure.

**For a household managing both an infant's ambient sound needs and a toddler's story content on a tight budget, is there a way to avoid buying two separate ecosystems entirely?**
Yes, in the specific case of Yoto: since its ambient/radio function is free and independent of the card-based Club system, a single Yoto Player (or a Yoto Player plus a Yoto Mini for the second room, as covered in [our two-bedroom hardware guide](/reviews/digital-services/yoto-two-bedroom-bluetooth-range-test)) can cover both the infant's ambient-sound needs and the toddler's story-content needs without maintaining two separate hardware ecosystems — see [our full library curation guide](/reviews/digital-services/shared-audio-library-infant-toddler) for how to structure that shared library across both children's stages.
